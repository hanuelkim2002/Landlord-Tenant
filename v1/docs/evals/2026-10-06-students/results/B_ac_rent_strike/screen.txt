K.D. Lewis Enterprises Corp. v. Smith, 445 So. 2d 1032 (Fla. 5th DCA 1984) [not verified]
  The court read: 83.60, Florida Statutes (1981); 83.60(1), Fla.Stats. (1981); 83.60(2), Florida Statutes (1981); 83.56, Florida Statutes (1981); 83.56(1), Florida Statutes (1981).
  Facts:
    "The tenants withheld payment of rent claiming that the landlord did not maintain their apartments and that a rental increase was unfair." (at 1033)
    "The landlord moved to require the tenants to deposit the rent into the registry of the court pursuant to section 83.60(2), Florida Statutes (1981). When they failed to do so, the court issued writs of possession against Smith and Lang and entered a judgment of possession against Williams who had already vacated the premises." (at 1033)
    "In Lang’s case, clothes in the amount of $300 were damaged because of a leak in her closet. Williams testified she drove her car into a pothole in the parking area causing $100 in damage." (at 1037)
  Question: Could the tenants recover the lost value of their apartments in negligence without the written notice in 83.56, and did the evidence support the compensatory awards?
  The issue in the court's words: "The next question is whether the evidence supports a finding of compensatory damages." (at 1037)
  Rule:
    "In summary, we reject the tenants’ contention that they could recover damages under a negligence theory for the diminution in value of their premises." (at 1038)
    "Of course, where a tenant or his property is injured by the negligence of the landlord, his cause of action requires no notice under the statute." (at 1038)
    "However, since there was some evidence of damage to the property of Lang and Williams, and the jury allowed a set-off in the exact amount of the compensatory damages, we affirm those awards. As to Smith, we hold that the trial court should have directed a verdict in favor of the landlord on the negligence count." (at 1038)
  Reasoning:
    "We conclude, however, that the tenants are incorrect and that they are arguing a contract theory of recovery rather than tort." (at 1037)
    "To recover for diminishment in the value of the premises, it is necessary for the tenant to comply with section 83.56, Florida Statutes (1981), which provides as follows" (at 1037)
    "To accept the tenants’ argument that the notice under section 83.56 is not required because they framed their claim in negligence would defeat the legislative requirement of notice." (at 1038)
  Other cases on it:
    Distinguishes Mansur v. Eubanks: "First, Mansur was not a tenant in the unit where he was injured. Second, the court merely abandoned the doctrine of caveat lessee. It did not create a new cause of action in tort for the diminution in value of the premises." (at 1038)

Short answer: on your facts, holding back rent is not safe, and the landlord can start an eviction over it. The law gives a tenant a defense in an eviction case, not a right to keep rent. That defense is not shown to be open to you yet.

Is the rent defense open? Not known yet. It reaches only a failure of a duty in Fla. Stat. 83.51(1) (Fla. Stat. 83.60(1)(b)). You confirmed the problem is not the structure or the plumbing, so the way in is a building, housing, or health code that requires the landlord to fix it (Fla. Stat. 83.51(1)(a)). The tool does not hold those codes. The city or county code enforcement office can tell you whether one covers this, and can inspect the unit. If one does, this defense can open. If none does, it is closed. Your lease promising to keep the AC in repair (Lease Section 7.1) does not change that, because the defense in 83.60(1)(b) speaks only of 83.51(1).

There is a second step. The defense can be raised only after 7 days have passed since you delivered a written notice "specifying the noncompliance and indicating the intention of the tenant not to pay rent by reason thereof" (Fla. Stat. 83.60(1)(b)). You confirmed your e-mail did not say you would not pay, so that step is still to do.

Can they evict you? Yes, that is the landlord's route for unpaid rent. After a written demand for the rent or possession, if you do not pay within 3 days (excluding Saturday, Sunday, and legal holidays), the landlord may end the lease (Fla. Stat. 83.56(3)). No such demand is in your record yet, and you confirmed no eviction case has been filed.

If the defense did hold (a code covers it and the notice step is done), a material noncompliance by the landlord "is a complete defense to an action for possession based upon nonpayment of rent", and the court decides how much the rent is reduced (Fla. Stat. 83.60(1)(b)). The statute does not settle what "material" means:
- One reading: a failure of a duty 83.51(1) lists is material whatever its size. Then a covered AC failure counts.
- Other reading: it turns on degree, meaning how serious the condition is, how long it has lasted, and how it affects living in the unit. Then a court would weigh an AC that has been out since Sept 22, fixed for one day, and still broken, in a hot unit.
The tool picks neither.

If the landlord does file, there is a hard rule. A tenant who raises any defense other than payment must pay the rent into the court registry within 5 days (excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays) after service of process, and the rent that falls due while the case runs, or file a motion to determine the amount within those 5 days (Fla. Stat. 83.60(2)). The tool counts calendar days only and does not work out that date.

What courts have said. These cut against a tenant who holds back rent and does not pay into the registry:
- K.D. Lewis Enterprises Corp. v. Smith, 445 So. 2d 1032 (Fla. 5th DCA 1984, reading the 1981 statute). Like you, tenants withheld rent over maintenance, but the landlord had already sued. The court upheld writs of possession "upon the refusal of Smith and Lang to deposit rent as required by the statute" (at 1035). The tenant loses "only his right to retain possession", not the claim against the landlord (at 1035). It also said that to recover the lost value of the premises a tenant must follow the written notice in 83.56 (at 1037). First Hanover follows it, and it distinguishes Mansur v. Eubanks.
- First Hanover v. Vazquez, 848 So. 2d 1188 (Fla. 3d DCA 2003, 2002 statute). Tenants with their own claims against the landlord were sued for possession for unpaid rent. Tenants "are obligated to pay rent as a condition to remaining in possession irrespective of their defenses and counterclaims" (at 1190). Their claim was fraud, yours is a repair, but 83.60(2) says "any defense other than payment".

These are courts' words in other people's cases, each with the statute version it read. How far one reaches your situation turns on how alike its facts are to yours.

Is it legal or not? The tool cannot tell you yes. The statute does not say a tenant may keep rent. It gives a defense if the landlord sues to evict you for unpaid rent, and on your facts that defense is not shown to be open yet. Until it is, not paying leaves you open to an eviction for nonpayment.

The code point, in plain words. A building, housing, or health code is a set of local rules, from the city or county, on the minimum condition of rental homes. The statute makes the landlord follow them (Fla. Stat. 83.51(1)(a)). The rent defense reaches only the duties in 83.51(1) (Fla. Stat. 83.60(1)(b)). The statute's own lists name the structure, the plumbing, and, in 83.51(2)(a), heat in winter, running water, and hot water. They do not name air conditioning, and you confirmed this is not the structure or the plumbing. So the AC is covered by the defense only if a local code requires it. The tool does not hold those codes. The code enforcement office for the city or county where your unit is can tell you, and can inspect the unit. That is a point you can check. If a code covers it, the rent defense can open once a notice saying you will not pay rent has been delivered and 7 days have passed (Fla. Stat. 83.60(1)(b)). If no code does, it is closed.

What your lease changes. It does say the landlord must keep the AC in good repair (Lease Section 7.1), and it treats a loss of AC above 85 degrees as an emergency (Lease Section 7.2). That does not open the rent defense, because 83.60(1)(b) speaks of 83.51(1) and not of a lease promise alone. It does open another option, ending the lease. You can end it if the landlord materially fails to fix the problem within 7 days after you deliver a written notice naming it and saying you will end the lease over it (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)). That section reaches "material provisions of the rental agreement", and the repair promise is one. Your e-mail did not say you would end the lease, so that step is still to do. What "material" means is not settled: one reading counts any failure of a listed duty, and the other turns on how serious the problem is, how long it has lasted, and how it affects living in the unit.

Should you pay now? That is your choice. Here is what each way turns on.
- Paying: there is then no unpaid rent for a 3-day demand to rest on (Fla. Stat. 83.56(3)). But a tenant who "pays rent with actual knowledge of a noncompliance by the landlord" waives the right "to terminate the rental agreement or to bring a civil action for that noncompliance, but not for any subsequent or continuing noncompliance" (Fla. Stat. 83.56(5)(a)). The statute does not settle how that applies to you. One reading: an AC that is still broken is a continuing noncompliance, so paying does not waive ending the lease. The other: the noncompliance is the failure to fix a problem you already knew of, so paying waives ending the lease over it, and only a new or different failure starts over.
- Not paying: the defense is open only if a code covers the AC, a notice saying you will not pay has been delivered, and 7 days have passed. If the landlord sues first, you must pay the rent into the court registry within 5 days (excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays) after service, or you lose the defense (Fla. Stat. 83.60(2)).

Do you have to pay the $50 late fee? Your lease says you do if rent is not received by 11:59 p.m. on the fifth day of the month: "a late charge of $50.00, plus $10.00 for each later day, not to exceed $150.00 in any month" (Lease Section 3.2). So the charge can grow while rent stays unpaid. I searched the statute text and found no section on late fees. Would the charge fall away if the rent defense held? The lease and statute text I have do not answer that.

Courts on the points above. Paying late and waiver:
- Multach v. Adams, 418 So. 2d 1254 (Fla. 4th DCA 1982, 1981 statute). A commercial lease, no rent paid. Refusing a late tender "did not constitute a waiver or a forgiveness of the debt" (at 1255), so paying late does not wipe out what is owed. The court found the statute it read, which it said applies "only to the rental of a dwelling unit", did not fit that commercial lease (at 1256). Yours is a home, so 83.56(5)(a) does apply.

The landlord's duty to repair. These are mostly injury cases, so they match you only on the landlord's duty:
- Mansur v. Eubanks, 401 So. 2d 1328 (Fla. 1981, 1977 statute). After the tenant takes possession, the landlord has "a continuing duty to exercise reasonable care to repair dangerous defective conditions upon notice of their existence by the tenant, unless waived by the tenant" (at 1330). It was a gas explosion. A broken AC is not shown to be dangerous, so this duty may not reach it. K.D. Lewis distinguishes it on lost rental value (at 1038).
- Bennett v. Mattison, 382 So. 2d 873 (Fla. 1st DCA 1980) and Paterson v. Deeb, 472 So. 2d 1210 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985, 1981 statute). Breaking the statute's duties "would be evidence of negligence" (Bennett at 875), "at least evidence of negligence" (Paterson at 1216). A hallway fall and broken locks. You report no injury, so they say nothing on your rent.
- Bennett M. Lifter, Inc. v. Varnado, 480 So. 2d 1336 (Fla. 3d DCA 1985, 1983 statute). A water heater was out three days after the tenant reported it, and a child was scalded. The duty "corresponds to the statutory warranty of habitability" and hot running water (at 1341). Hot water is on the statute's list, and AC is not. Smith v. Grove Apartments follows it.
- Solomon v. Gentry, 388 So. 2d 52 (Fla. 4th DCA 1980, 1979 statute). "No such warranty arises under a lease of real property" (at 53), as to an implied warranty of fitness. Here the promise is written in your lease, so you do not need an implied one.
- Menendez v. Palms West Condo. Ass'n, 736 So. 2d 58 (Fla. 1st DCA 1999). No lease liability where "the lease contained no provision" requiring the item (at 62). Your lease does contain a repair promise for the AC. It also distinguishes Paterson, whose duty rested on an express statutory provision.
- Smith v. Grove Apartments, LLC, 976 So. 2d 582 (Fla. 3d DCA 2007, 2007 statute). The landlord "retained its statutory duty to maintain the parking lot area" after repeated complaints (at 586). A common area, not your unit's AC.
- Haynes v. Lloyd, 533 So. 2d 944 (Fla. 5th DCA 1988). The common-areas statute "merely restates the landlord's common law duty" (at 945). Broken glass, not on point.
- Ray v. Tampa Windridge Associates, 596 So. 2d 676 (Fla. 2d DCA 1991, 1985 statute). A signed release did not remove the landlord's duty to make reasonable provisions for locks (at 677). Locks and a release, not an AC promise.
