Bennett v. Mattison, 382 So. 2d 873 (Fla. 1st DCA 1980) [not verified]
  The court read: 83.51(2)(a) 3, F.S. (1977); 83.51(2)(a) (no year stated; in the special concurrence).
  Facts:
    "Melena Bennett, a tenant and part-time employee of the Casa Marina Apartments, slipped and fell in a hallway at the apartment complex on July 9, 1978." (at 874)
    "Both the hallway and the patio were provided for the common use and benefit of the tenants." (at 874)
    "The apartment manager testified that she had received numerous complaints from tenants about the slippery condition of the hallway when it was wet and that she had actually seen people slip in the hallway before Melena’s accident." (at 874)
  Question: What bearing does 83.51(2)(a) 3 have on a negligence claim over a fall in a common hallway?
  Rule:
    "Since both the hallway and patio were provided for the common use and benefit of the tenants, appellees were required by Section 83.51(2)(a) 3, F.S. (1977) to keep these areas safe and clean." (at 875)
    "The violation of this statutory duty would be evidence of negligence." (at 875)
  Reasoning: none stated beyond the statute it cites.
  Other cases on it:
    Cites, on a statutory violation as evidence of negligence de Jesus v. Seaboard Coast Line Railroad Company, 281 So. 2d 198 (not in `cases/`): "The violation of this statutory duty would be evidence of negligence. See de Jesus v. Seaboard Coast Line Railroad Company, 281 So.2d 198 (Fla.1973)." (at 875)
    Cites, on notice of a recurring condition as a jury question Manassa v. New Hampshire Insurance Company, 332 So. 2d 34 (not in `cases/`)
    The special concurrence cites it for the common law rule it reads the statute to remove, in part Brooks v. Peters, 157 Fla. 141, 25 So. 2d 205 (not in `cases/`): "See Brooks v. Peters, 157 Fla. 141, 25 So.2d 205 (1946)" (at 875)
    The special concurrence cites Judge Ervin's concurrence in the First District decision later reviewed in Mansur v. Eubanks, 401 So. 2d 1328 Mansur v. Eubanks (Fla. 1st DCA), 368 So. 2d 645 (not in `cases/`): "For a more exhaustive study of the problem, see Mansur v. Eubanks, 368 So.2d 645, 647 (Fla. 1st DCA 1978) (Ervin, J., concurring)." (at 876)
    Paterson v. Deeb cites, on a violation of the act as evidence of negligence and on the sufficiency of the complaint this case: "A landlord’s violation of the act is at least evidence of negligence. Bennett v. Mattison, 382 So.2d 873 (Fla. 1st DCA 1980)" (Paterson v. Deeb at 1216)
    Lockamy v. Byrne: a footnote in the dissent cites this case on a violation of the Act as evidence of negligence: "I am also aware that a landlord’s violation of the provisions of the Florida Residential Landlord and Tenant Act has been characterized as evidence of negligence." Paterson v. Deeb, 472 So. 2d 1210 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985) [not verified]
  The court read: sections 83.40 through 83.63, Florida Statutes (1981); 83.51, Florida Statutes (1981); 83.51(2)(a), Florida Statutes (1981); 83.41, Fla.Stat.   Facts:

  Question: Did the amended complaint state a cause of action in negligence for the attack, though it alleged no prior similar crime on the premises?
  The issue in the court's words: "(1) Whether the allegations were sufficient to allege a cause of action for negligence" (at 1214)
  Rule:
    "Since the purpose of the statutory duty to provide locks is the prevention of criminal acts by unauthorized persons having unrestrained access to the premises, we conclude that the landlord’s duty to reasonably provide protection from criminal attack does not have to be implied from prior similar occurrences on the leased premises." (at 1217)
    "In view of the obvious purpose of the statutory duty to provide locks and security to prevent potential damages caused by the acts of unwarranted trespassers on the leased premises, we expressly decline to require as the essential predicate to liability allegation and proof that the landlord had actual or constructive knowledge of prior similar criminal acts committed on the premises." (at 1218)
    "These allegations, considered with the alleged violations of the statutory duty to provide locks and keys and to maintain the common areas in a safe condition, are sufficient to state a cause of action in negligence." (at 1220)
  Reasoning:
    "It is clear that the Florida Residential and Landlord-Tenant Act imposes definite obligations upon the landlord which continue throughout the term of the lease and which did not exist under the common law." (at 1216)
    "Certainly the principal reason, if not the only reason, for furnishing locks and keys is to prevent a human being from making an unauthorized entry into the premises." (at 1217)
    "We are not willing to give the landlord one free ride, as it were, and sacrifice the first victim’s right to safety upon the altar of foreseeability by slavishly adhering to the now-discredited notion that at least one criminal assault must have occurred on the premises before the landlord can be held liable." (at 1218)
  Question: Is a landlord's violation of 83.51 negligence per se, or evidence of negligence?
  Rule:
    "A landlord’s violation of the act is at least evidence of negligence." (at 1216)
    In a footnote: "We do not consider this section to be a “strict liability statute” in the sense that it protects a particular class of persons from their inability to protect themselves" (at 1217)
  Reasoning:
    In the same footnote: "nor is it a penal statute designed to protect a particular class of persons from a particular injury or type of injury, as was the statute in De Jesus, since no criminal penalties are imposed for violating section 83.51." (at 1217)
  Question: Does the 83.51 duty to provide locks and keys reach the building's outside entrances and the hallway bathroom door?
  Rule:
    In a footnote: "Appellees argue that section 83.51 does not require the landlord to provide “locks and keys” for outside entrances to the building or the upstairs hallway bathroom door because these areas are not a part of the “dwelling unit.” We disagree." (at 1217)
  Reasoning:
    "The term “dwelling unit” is defined in section 83.43(2) to mean a structure or part of a structure that is rented for use as a residence, and is broad enough to include the bathroom and common areas." (at 1217)

The statute gives no set number of days for restoring hot water. Its only clock starts when you deliver a written notice, and the program's deadlines show none running, because you confirmed you have not delivered one.

Hot water is a duty the statute names for a unit that is not a single-family home or duplex, "Unless otherwise agreed in writing" (Fla. Stat. 83.51(2)(a)). You confirmed yours is not a house or duplex. The tool found nothing on hot water or a water heater in your lease's text. Your lease does put plumbing and air-conditioning repair on the landlord (Lease Section 7.1).

Pay less rent: open, as a defense if the landlord sues for possession (Fla. Stat. 83.60(1)(b)). It needs a written notice delivered to the landlord that names the problem and says you intend not to pay rent because of it, then 7 days. That notice is still to do. If the defense is made out, the court decides how much the rent is reduced (Fla. Stat. 83.60(1)(b)). If the landlord files a case, you must pay the rent into the court registry within 5 days, excluding Saturdays, Sundays, and legal holidays, after service to keep any defense other than payment (Fla. Stat. 83.60(2)). The tool does not compute that date.

End the lease: not settled (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)). It needs a written notice that names the problem and says you intend to end the lease, then 7 days without a fix. You confirmed you paid rent after you knew of the problem. The statute does not settle whether that waived ending the lease over a problem that is still not fixed (Fla. Stat. 83.56(5)(a)).
- Reading one: an unfixed problem is a continuing noncompliance, so paying does not waive it, and this option is open.
- Reading two: the noncompliance is the failure to fix a problem you already knew of, so paying waives it, and only a new or different failure starts over. Then this option is closed.

Two more points the statute does not settle reach both options.
- Which list the heater falls under. Fla. Stat. 83.56(1) and 83.60(1)(b) speak only of 83.51(1), which covers codes, structure, and plumbing, while hot water is named in 83.51(2)(a). On one reading, it is an 83.51(1) duty when a building, housing, or health code covers it (Fla. Stat. 83.51(3)). The tool does not hold those codes; your city or county code enforcement office can tell you. On the other, it is an 83.51(2) duty because that subsection names it, so neither notice route reaches it, and part II does not let you raise an 83.51(2) noncompliance as a defense to an action for possession (Fla. Stat. 83.51(2)(c)). On that reading the rent defense would not be open.
- What "material" means. Both 7-day periods need a landlord who "materially fails to comply" or a "material noncompliance" (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1), 83.60(1)(b)). The statute does not define it. On one reading, failing a duty 83.51(1) lists is material whatever its size. On the other, it turns on how serious the condition is, how long it has lasted, and how it affects living in the unit.

Your AC is separate. Your lease promises the landlord keeps the air-conditioning in good working order (Lease Section 7.1), and Fla. Stat. 83.56(1) also reaches "material provisions of the rental agreement", so that clause can support ending the lease over the AC. Fla. Stat. 83.60(1)(b) speaks only of 83.51(1), so a lease provision alone does not reach the rent defense.

Money back: the aggrieved party "may recover the damages caused by the noncompliance" (Fla. Stat. 83.55). The statute gives no formula and the tool computes no amount. Your lease says no interruption of services "entitles Resident to any reduction or abatement of Rent" when it is "caused by a provider, a repair, or anything beyond Landlord's reasonable control" (Lease Section 5.2). A lease term is "void and unenforceable" to the extent it waives the rights or remedies in this part or limits the landlord's liability under law (Fla. Stat. 83.47(1)). So Section 5.2 cannot take away what 83.60(1)(b) and 83.55 give. Whether it reaches a broken heater the landlord has not replaced is a point you can check against its words.
