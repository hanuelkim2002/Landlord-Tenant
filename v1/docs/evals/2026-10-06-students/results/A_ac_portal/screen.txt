K.D. Lewis Enterprises Corp. v. Smith, 445 So. 2d 1032 (Fla. 5th DCA 1984) [not verified]
  The court read: 83.60, Florida Statutes (1981); 83.60(1), Fla.Stats. (1981); 83.60(2), Florida Statutes (1981); 83.56, Florida Statutes (1981); 83.56(1), Florida Statutes (1981).
  Facts:
    "The tenants withheld payment of rent claiming that the landlord did not maintain their apartments and that a rental increase was unfair." (at 1033)
    "The landlord moved to require the tenants to deposit the rent into the registry of the court pursuant to section 83.60(2), Florida Statutes (1981). When they failed to do so, the court issued writs of possession against Smith and Lang and entered a judgment of possession against Williams who had already vacated the premises." (at 1033)
    "In Lang’s case, clothes in the amount of $300 were damaged because of a leak in her closet. Williams testified she drove her car into a pothole in the parking area causing $100 in damage." (at 1037)
  Question: Could the tenants recover the lost value of their apartments in negligence without the written notice in 83.56, and did the evidence support the compensatory awards?
  The issue in the court's words: "The next question is whether the evidence supports a finding of compensatory damages." (at 1037)
  Rule:
    "In summary, we reject the tenants’ contention that they could recover damages under a negligence theory for the diminution in value of their premises." (at 1038)
    "Of course, where a tenant or his property is injured by the negligence of the landlord, his cause of action requires no notice under the statute." (at 1038)
    "However, since there was some evidence of damage to the property of Lang and Williams, and the jury allowed a set-off in the exact amount of the compensatory damages, we affirm those awards. As to Smith, we hold that the trial court should have directed a verdict in favor of the landlord on the negligence count." (at 1038)
  Reasoning:
    "We conclude, however, that the tenants are incorrect and that they are arguing a contract theory of recovery rather than tort." (at 1037)
    "To recover for diminishment in the value of the premises, it is necessary for the tenant to comply with section 83.56, Florida Statutes (1981), which provides as follows" (at 1037)
    "To accept the tenants’ argument that the notice under section 83.56 is not required because they framed their claim in negligence would defeat the legislative requirement of notice." (at 1038)
  Other cases on it:
    Distinguishes Mansur v. Eubanks: "First, Mansur was not a tenant in the unit where he was injured. Second, the court merely abandoned the doctrine of caveat lessee. It did not create a new cause of action in tort for the diminution in value of the premises." (at 1038)
    Limits to its facts Freedman v. Geiger, 314 So. 2d 189 (not in `cases/`): "While this language is capable of the construction urged by the tenants, we think the holding should be limited to its facts." (at 1036)
    Cites, on the standard for punitive damages, Winn & Lovett Grocery Co. v. Archer, 126 Fla. 308 (not in `cases/`)
    First Hanover v. Vazquez follows, and quotes at length this case: "We find the same analysis applicable to the instant dispute." (First Hanover v. Vazquez at 1191)

Paterson v. Deeb, 472 So. 2d 1210 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985) [not verified]
  The court read: sections 83.40 through 83.63, Florida Statutes (1981); 83.51, Florida Statutes (1981); 83.51(2)(a), Florida Statutes (1981); 83.41, Fla.Stat. (1981); 83.43(2) (no year stated); 83.44, Fla.Stat. (1981); 83.54, Fla.Stat. (1981); 83.55, Fla.Stat. (1981); 83.55 as the Florida Law Revision Council recommended it, and ch. 73-330, Laws of Florida (1973).
  Facts:
    "The hallways and stairwell were common areas under the sole control of defendants and were poorly lighted." (at 1213)
    "She specifically complained that the only lock to the front door of the building was broken and that the rear door of the building had no lock or other security device." (at 1213)
    "Defendants’ rental agent discussed plaintiff’s requests with defendants and informed plaintiff that her requests for repairs and improved security would not be met because the building was to be demolished and improvements were impractical as a waste of money to defendants." (at 1213)
  Question: Did the amended complaint state a cause of action in negligence for the attack, though it alleged no prior similar crime on the premises?
  The issue in the court's words: "(1) Whether the allegations were sufficient to allege a cause of action for negligence" (at 1214)
  Rule:
    "Since the purpose of the statutory duty to provide locks is the prevention of criminal acts by unauthorized persons having unrestrained access to the premises, we conclude that the landlord’s duty to reasonably provide protection from criminal attack does not have to be implied from prior similar occurrences on the leased premises." (at 1217)
    "In view of the obvious purpose of the statutory duty to provide locks and security to prevent potential damages caused by the acts of unwarranted trespassers on the leased premises, we expressly decline to require as the essential predicate to liability allegation and proof that the landlord had actual or constructive knowledge of prior similar criminal acts committed on the premises." (at 1218)
    "These allegations, considered with the alleged violations of the statutory duty to provide locks and keys and to maintain the common areas in a safe condition, are sufficient to state a cause of action in negligence." (at 1220)
  Reasoning:
    "It is clear that the Florida Residential and Landlord-Tenant Act imposes definite obligations upon the landlord which continue throughout the term of the lease and which did not exist under the common law." (at 1216)
    "Certainly the principal reason, if not the only reason, for furnishing locks and keys is to prevent a human being from making an unauthorized entry into the premises." (at 1217)
    "We are not willing to give the landlord one free ride, as it were, and sacrifice the first victim’s right to safety upon the altar of foreseeability by slavishly adhering to the now-discredited notion that at least one criminal assault must have occurred on the premises before the landlord can be held liable." (at 1218)
  Question: Is a landlord's violation of 83.51 negligence per se, or evidence of negligence?
  Rule:
    "A landlord’s violation of the act is at least evidence of negligence." (at 1216)
    In a footnote: "We do not consider this section to be a “strict liability statute” in the sense that it protects a particular class of persons from their inability to protect themselves" (at 1217)
  Reasoning:
    In the same footnote: "nor is it a penal statute designed to protect a particular class of persons from a particular injury or type of injury, as was the statute in De Jesus, since no criminal penalties are imposed for violating section 83.51." (at 1217)

  Reasoning:
    "The term “dwelling unit” is defined in section 83.43(2) to mean a structure or part of a structure that is rented for use as a residence, and is broad enough to include the bathroom and common areas." (at 1217)

These are courts' words in other people's cases, each with the statute version it read. How far one reaches your situation turns on how alike its facts are to yours.

Neither option is open on what is known now. Ending the lease is not settled, and not paying rent is not known yet. Both are about ending the lease or holding back rent; neither makes the landlord do the repair itself.

Ending the lease over the repair: not settled.
If the landlord "materially fails" to fix the problem within 7 days after delivery of your written notice naming it and saying you intend to end the lease because of it, you may end the lease (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)). Your lease says the landlord shall maintain the air-conditioning (Lease Section 7.1), and you confirmed you did not cause the problem, so the landlord's duty is met.

The open point is rent. You confirmed you paid rent after you knew the AC was out. A tenant who "pays rent with actual knowledge of a noncompliance by the landlord" waives ending the lease over it, "but not for any subsequent or continuing noncompliance" (Fla. Stat. 83.56(5)(a)). The statute does not settle this:
- One reading: a problem still not fixed is a continuing noncompliance, so paying rent while it goes on does not waive ending the lease. Then this option is open.
- Other reading: the noncompliance is the failure to fix the problem you already knew of, so paying rent waived ending the lease over it, and only a new or different failure starts over. Then this option is closed.
The statute also does not define "materially" (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)). It may turn on how serious the condition is, how long it has lasted, and how it affects living in the unit, or on whether the duty is one 83.51(1) lists, whatever its size.

Not paying rent as a defense: not known yet.
If the landlord sues for possession over unpaid rent, you may defend on a "material noncompliance with s. 83.51 (1)" once 7 days have passed after your written notice saying you intend not to pay rent because of the problem (Fla. Stat. 83.60(1)(b)). A lease promise alone does not reach this defense. You confirmed the AC is not structure or plumbing, which is one way in (Fla. Stat. 83.51(1)(b)). The other is a building, housing, or health code where your unit is that requires the landlord to fix it (Fla. Stat. 83.51(1)(a)). This tool does not hold those codes. You can check: the city or county code enforcement office can say whether one covers this problem, and can inspect the unit. If a code covers it, this option is open; if not, it is closed. You confirmed no eviction case has been filed; once one is, a tenant raising "any defense other than payment" must pay the rent into the court registry (Fla. Stat. 83.60(2)).

What either option needs first.
You confirmed your portal request did not say you intend to end the lease or not to pay rent, so neither 7-day period has started. Each starts from a new written notice delivered to the landlord that names the problem and says one of those intentions. Then 7 days must pass after it is delivered.

How the notice is delivered matters. Your lease says a maintenance request "is not by itself a notice under Section 13.1" (Lease Section 7.2). Section 13.1 lists delivery by hand to the leasing office, certified mail, or e-mail to office@magnoliarow.example (Lease Section 13.1). The statute lists "mailing, delivering a true copy thereof, e-mailing" (Fla. Stat. 83.56(4)). The program counts your portal request as a delivered written notice because you confirmed it, but whether it counts is for you to check against Lease Section 13.1.

Your lease also treats "Loss of air-conditioning when the outside temperature is above 85 degrees" as an emergency, reported by calling the after-hours emergency line at (352) 555-0147 (Lease Section 7.2). That turns on the outside temperature, not the temperature in your room, which you can check.

The cases on "Tenant's notice of the landlord's noncompliance" are printed above in this chat.

Getting rent back is not one of the options the program checks, and the statute text I have does not settle it on your facts. It can come up three ways, and each turns on a point that is not decided yet.

1. Damages. "The aggrieved party may recover the damages caused by the noncompliance" when a landlord fails to comply with the rental agreement or this part (Fla. Stat. 83.55). Your lease says the landlord shall maintain the air-conditioning (Lease Section 7.1). This is not settled, because you confirmed you paid rent after you knew of the problem, and a tenant who does so waives the right "to bring a civil action for that noncompliance, but not for any subsequent or continuing noncompliance" (Fla. Stat. 83.56(5)(a)). The statute does not settle this:
- One reading: a problem still not fixed is a continuing noncompliance, so paying rent while it goes on does not waive a claim over it. Then this route is open.
- Other reading: the failure to fix the problem you already knew of is the noncompliance, so paying rent waived a claim over it, and only a new or different failure starts over. Then this route is closed.

2. A rent reduction. The statute says that where a failure is due to causes beyond the landlord's control, the landlord "has made and continues to make every reasonable effort", and the unit is still lived in, "the rent for the period of noncompliance shall be reduced by an amount in proportion to the loss of rental value caused by the noncompliance" (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)(b)). Whether the delay for a part is beyond the landlord's control, and whether the landlord has made every reasonable effort, are not known yet. That sentence sits inside the one about a failure after a tenant's notice, and the statute text I have does not say whether it reaches a tenant whose notice stated no intention to end the lease.

3. A reduction as a defense, only if the landlord sues for possession over unpaid rent. Then "the amount, if any, by which the rent is to be reduced to reflect the diminution in value of the dwelling unit" is for the court or jury to decide (Fla. Stat. 83.60(1)(b)). This needs a failure of a duty in 83.51(1), which is not known yet (whether a code where your unit is covers an AC; the city or county code enforcement office can tell you), and a notice saying you intend not to pay rent, which you said you do not want to send.

Your lease points the other way on a reduction: "no such interruption entitles Resident to any reduction or abatement of Rent", for an "interruption or failure of utilities, internet, or other services caused by a provider, a repair, or anything beyond Landlord's reasonable control" (Lease Section 5.2). Whether an AC with a dead compressor is such a service is for you to check against that clause. The statute says a rental agreement provision is "void and unenforceable to the extent that it" "Purports to waive or preclude the rights, remedies, or requirements set forth in this part" (Fla. Stat. 83.47(1)(a)). So if a reduction is one of the statute's remedies on your facts, Lease Section 5.2 is void to that extent; if it is not, 5.2 is not what stands in the way.
