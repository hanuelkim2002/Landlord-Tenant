The statute sets how a claim is made. The landlord must give written notice of its claim, by certified mail or e-mail, within 30 days after the rental agreement ends, and you then have 15 days after you receive it to object in writing (Fla. Stat. 83.49(3)(a)). A landlord that misses the 30 days "forfeits the right to impose a claim upon the security deposit," but may still sue for damages after returning it (Fla. Stat. 83.49(3)(a)). If you do not object in time, the landlord may deduct the claim, and that "does not waive any rights of the tenant to seek damages in a separate action" (Fla. Stat. 83.49(3)(b)). This changes only if you move out before the term ends. Whether it applies turns on your lease's own term on notice before moving out (Lease Section 9.2). If it applies and you do not give that notice, the landlord is relieved of the claim notice (Fla. Stat. 83.49(5)).

A landlord that missed the deposit claim notice "forfeited his access" to the deposit, which "may not be used for purposes of a set-off," though it could still bring a separate claim for damages. Malagon v. Solari, 566 So. 2d 352, 354 (Fla. 4th DCA 1990), read the 1989 text and follows Durene. Damages the landlord won on a counterclaim could not be a setoff against the deposit where the landlord had not given the required notice, and it noted the landlord's liability for fees under 83.49(3)(c). Ending the lease (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)) is not settled. What stays open is whether paying rent waived this (Fla. Stat. 83.56(5)(a)). The statute does not settle it. One reading: a leak the landlord still has not fixed is a continuing noncompliance, so paying rent does not waive ending the lease, and the option is open. The other: the noncompliance is the failure to fix a problem you already knew of, so paying rent with that knowledge waives it, and the option is closed. The statute also does not define "material," so whether a small leak is one is not settled either.
