K.D. Lewis Enterprises Corp. v. Smith, 445 So. 2d 1032 (Fla. 5th DCA 1984) [not verified]
  The court read: 83.60, Florida Statutes (1981); 83.60(1), Fla.Stats. (1981); 83.60(2), Florida Statutes (1981); 83.56, Florida Statutes (1981); 83.56(1), Florida Statutes (1981).
  Facts:
    "The tenants withheld payment of rent claiming that the landlord did not maintain their apartments and that a rental increase was unfair." (at 1033)
    "The landlord moved to require the tenants to deposit the rent into the registry of the court pursuant to section 83.60(2), Florida Statutes (1981). When they failed to do so, the court issued writs of possession against Smith and Lang and entered a judgment of possession against Williams who had already vacated the premises." (at 1033)
    "In Lang’s case, clothes in the amount of $300 were damaged because of a leak in her closet. Williams testified she drove her car into a pothole in the parking area causing $100 in damage." (at 1037)
  Question: Could the tenants recover the lost value of their apartments in negligence without the written notice in 83.56, and did the evidence support the compensatory awards?
  The issue in the court's words: "The next question is whether the evidence supports a finding of compensatory damages." (at 1037)
  Rule:
    "In summary, we reject the tenants’ contention that they could recover damages under a negligence theory for the diminution in value of their premises." (at 1038)
    "Of course, where a tenant or his property is injured by the negligence of the landlord, his cause of action requires no notice under the statute." (at 1038)
    "However, since there was some evidence of damage to the property of Lang and Williams, and the jury allowed a set-off in the exact amount of the compensatory damages, we affirm those awards. As to Smith, we hold that the trial court should have directed a verdict in favor of the landlord on the negligence count." (at 1038)
  Reasoning:
    "We conclude, however, that the tenants are incorrect and that they are arguing a contract theory of recovery rather than tort." (at 1037)
    "To recover for diminishment in the value of the premises, it is necessary for the tenant to comply with section 83.56, Florida Statutes (1981), which provides as follows" (at 1037)
    "To accept the tenants’ argument that the notice under section 83.56 is not required because they framed their claim in negligence would defeat the legislative requirement of notice." (at 1038)
  Other cases on it:
    Distinguishes Mansur v. Eubanks: "First, Mansur was not a tenant in the unit where he was injured. Second, the court merely abandoned the doctrine of caveat lessee. It did not create a new cause of action in tort for the diminution in value of the premises." (at 1038)
    Limits to its facts Freedman v. Geiger, 314 So. 2d 189 (not in `cases/`): "While this language is capable of the construction urged by the tenants, we think the holding should be limited to its facts." (at 1036)
    Cites, on the standard for punitive damages, Winn & Lovett Grocery Co. v. Archer, 126 Fla. 308 (not in `cases/`)
    First Hanover v. Vazquez follows, and quotes at length this case: "We find the same analysis applicable to the instant dispute." (First Hanover v. Vazquez at 1191)

Paterson v. Deeb, 472 So. 2d 1210 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985) [not verified]
  The court read: sections 83.40 through 83.63, Florida Statutes (1981); 83.51, Florida Statutes (1981); 83.51(2)(a), Florida Statutes (1981); 83.41, Fla.Stat. (1981); 83.43(2) (no year stated); 83.44, Fla.Stat. (1981); 83.54, Fla.Stat. (1981); 83.55, Fla.Stat.   Facts:
    "The hallways and stairwell were common areas under the sole control of defendants and were poorly lighted." (at 1213)
    "She specifically complained that the only lock to the front door of the building was broken and that the rear door of the building had no lock or other security device." (at 1213)
    "Defendants’ rental agent discussed plaintiff’s requests with defendants and informed plaintiff that her requests for repairs and improved security would not be met because the building was to be demolished and improvements were impractical as a waste of money to defendants." (at 1213)
  Question: Did the amended complaint state a cause of action in negligence for the attack, though it alleged no prior similar crime on the premises?
  The issue in the court's words: "(1) Whether the allegations were sufficient to allege a cause of action for negligence" (at 1214)
  Rule:
    "Since the purpose of the statutory duty to provide locks is the prevention of criminal acts by unauthorized persons having unrestrained access to the premises, we conclude that the landlord’s duty to reasonably provide protection from criminal attack does not have to be implied from prior similar occurrences on the leased premises." (at 1217)
    "In view of the obvious purpose of the statutory duty to provide locks and security to prevent potential damages caused by the acts of unwarranted trespassers on the leased premises, we expressly decline to require as the essential predicate to liability allegation and proof that the landlord had actual or constructive knowledge of prior similar criminal acts committed on the premises." (at 1218)
    "These allegations, considered with the alleged violations of the statutory duty to provide locks and keys and to maintain the common areas in a safe condition, are sufficient to state a cause of action in negligence." (at 1220)
  Reasoning:
    "It is clear that the Florida Residential and Landlord-Tenant Act imposes definite obligations upon the landlord which continue throughout the term of the lease and which did not exist under the common law." (at 1216)
    "Certainly the principal reason, if not the only reason, for furnishing locks and keys is to prevent a human being from making an unauthorized entry into the premises." (at 1217)
    "We are not willing to give the landlord one free ride, as it were, and sacrifice the first victim’s right to safety upon the altar of foreseeability by slavishly adhering to the now-discredited notion that at least one criminal assault must have occurred on the premises before the landlord can be held liable." (at 1218)
  Question: Is a landlord's violation of 83.51 negligence per se, or evidence of negligence?
  Rule:
    "A landlord’s violation of the act is at least evidence of negligence." (at 1216)
    In a footnote: "We do not consider this section to be a “strict liability statute” in the sense that it protects a particular class of persons from their inability to protect themselves" (at 1217)
  Reasoning:
    In the same footnote: "nor is it a penal statute designed to protect a particular class of persons from a particular injury or type of injury, as was the statute in De Jesus, since no criminal penalties are imposed for violating section 83.51." (at 1217)
  Question: Does the 83.51 duty to provide locks and keys reach the building's outside entrances and the hallway bathroom door?
  Rule:
    In a footnote: "Appellees argue that section 83.51 does not require the landlord to provide “locks and keys” for outside entrances to the building or the upstairs hallway bathroom door because these areas are not a part of the “dwelling unit.” We disagree." (at 1217)
  Reasoning:
    "The term “dwelling unit” is defined in section 83.43(2) to mean a structure or part of a structure that is rented for use as a residence, and is broad enough to include the bathroom and common areas." (at 1217)

Whether you can leave over the AC and the mold, and skip the fee, is not settled yet. Here is each way out and where it stands.

1. Ending the lease because the repair was not made: not settled.
The rule: you may end the lease if the landlord "materially fails to comply" within 7 days after your written notice that names the problem and says you intend to end the lease over it (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)). Your lease promises this repair: the landlord shall "maintain in good working order and repair the heating, air-conditioning" (Lease Section 7.1).
Applied: the duty is met, the problem was not caused by you, and you confirmed your notice named it and said you intend to end the lease. The program's deadlines show the 7 days have passed. Two points are open.
- Paying rent. The statute says a tenant who "pays rent with actual knowledge of a noncompliance by the landlord" waives ending the lease, "but not for any subsequent or continuing noncompliance" (Fla. Stat. 83.56(5)(a)). You confirmed paying rent after you knew of the problem. The statute does not settle this. One reading: a problem still not fixed is a continuing noncompliance, so paying did not waive ending the lease. Other reading: the failure to fix the problem you already knew of is the noncompliance, so paying waived it, and only a new or different failure starts over. On the first reading this option is open. On the second it is closed.
- "Materially." The statute does not define it (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)). One reading: a failure of a duty that 83.51(1) lists counts whatever its size. Other reading: it turns on degree, meaning how serious the condition is, how long it has lasted, and how it affects living in the unit. You confirmed the AC is not a structure or plumbing item, and the tool does not hold local codes, so the first reading would turn on a code (see the second option). 83.56(1) also reaches "material provisions of the rental agreement," and your lease promises this repair. On the degree reading, the leak, the black spots, your asthma, and how long it has gone on weigh for you. The techs saying they need parts weighs the other way.
So: if the first reading of the rent point holds and the problem counts as material, you "may terminate the rental agreement" (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)), and no step is left, since the notice and the 7 days are done. The statute does not mention a fee there. If the second reading holds, this way out is closed.
One more point: if the wait for parts is a cause beyond the landlord's control and the landlord "continues to make every reasonable effort," a different part of the statute applies. If the problem makes the unit untenantable and you vacate, "the tenant shall not be liable for rent during the period the dwelling unit remains uninhabitable" (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)(a)). 1. Does the letter count as your notice, or do you need something else?
On its words, yes, for ending the lease. The statute asks for a written notice that names the problem and says you intend to end the lease because of it (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)). Your letter names the AC and the mold and says "I am ending my lease because the AC and the mold were not fixed after I told you." The statute lists "delivering a true copy" and e-mailing as ways to deliver it (Fla. Stat. 83.56(4)), and your lease lists by hand and e-mail (Lease Section 13.1). So you do not need a separate paper for that.
Three limits. First, you already gave a notice earlier. You confirmed it named the problem and said you intend to end the lease, and the program's deadlines show its 7 days have passed. This letter would be a second one. Check whether your earlier e-mail named the mold. If it did not, this letter is the first written notice naming the mold, and the 7 days for the mold would run from when it is delivered (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)). The program computes only from a notice date you confirmed. Second, the letter does not say you will stop paying rent, so it is not the notice for that option (Fla. Stat. 83.60(1)(b)). Third, a good notice does not settle the two open points from before: whether paying rent waived ending the lease, and whether the problem is "material." Ending the lease over the repair is still not settled.
Cases: Lakeway Management Co. of Florida v. Stolowilsky, 527 So. 2d 950 (Fla. 3d DCA 1988), treated a written complaint as the condition before a tenant who left could argue constructive eviction (at 951). K.D. Lewis Enterprises Corp. v. Smith, 445 So. 2d 1032 (Fla. 5th DCA 1984), said a tenant claiming lost value must comply with 83.56 (at 1037). Both printed above. Yours has a written complaint, so you are on the right side of both. Neither says a notice alone lets you leave.

2. What happens if you hand it in tomorrow and move out on the date on file?
The program's deadlines show the last day to give the 7-day notice before that move-out date has passed. The tool does not count the days itself. So a letter handed in tomorrow would be late for that date.
The $300: not settled. Your lease says you "shall forfeit the security deposit" (Lease Section 9.2). The statute says missing the notice "does not waive any right the tenant may have to the security deposit or any part of it" (Fla. Stat. 83.49(5)). One reading: the lease may set its own term, since 83.49(5) opens with "Except when otherwise provided by the terms of a written rental agreement," so the deposit is lost. Other reading: a lease term that waives rights in this part is void "to the extent that it" does so (Fla. Stat. 83.47(1)(a)), so the statute's line governs and you keep your rights to the deposit. The text I have does not pick one.
Something worse than the $300? The lease says the deposit is what a missed notice costs (Lease Section 9.2). The text I have names no other result for it. Whether a missed notice is itself a further breach, that text does not answer. Separately, whether leaving breaks the lease turns on the repair route, which is not settled. If it holds, the lease ends (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)). If it does not, you left before the term ended, and the landlord chooses among the remedies in 83.595, including "holding the lessee liable for the rent as it comes due" (Fla. Stat. 83.595(3)). A late notice does not appear in 83.595.
Cases, all printed above, and they cut against you:

The landlord won a judgment for the rent. Yours differs in that you gave written notice of the problem, but a missed 7-day notice can let a landlord apply the deposit without the claim notice.
