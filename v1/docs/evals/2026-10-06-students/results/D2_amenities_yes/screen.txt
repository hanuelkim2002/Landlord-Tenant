Fuentes v. Owen, 310 So. 2d 458 (Fla. 3d DCA 1975) [not verified]
  The court read: 83.47, Fla. Stat. (ch. 73-330, s. 2) (no year stated).
  Facts:
    "The plaintiff and one Roland Tomassetti were the lessees of an apartment in a building owned by the defendant Lincoln Property Company No. Ten of Atlanta, Georgia, d/b/a Spanish Trace Club Apartments, herein referred to as the landlord, of which the defendant Michael W. Owen was the owners’ employed resident manager." (at 458)
    Paragraph 28 of the printed lease, in part: "Lessee agrees to release, indemnify and hold harmless the Lessor and the Agents from and against any and all claims for damage to persons or property occurring to Lessee, members of Lessee’s family or guests of Lessee, arising from the use or occupancy of the lease premises" (at 459)
    "The defendants moved to dismiss the complaint on the ground that the above quoted clause operated to relieve them of liability for the acts complained of." (at 459)
  Question: Was the lease's exculpatory clause void under a statute that took effect after the lease was made and the events occurred?
  The issue in the court's words: "In seeking reversal thereof the plaintiff contends first that the exculpatory clause in the lease should be held to be void by virtue of a statute so providing which was enacted and became effective agter the date of this lease and after the time of the occurrences upon which the complaint is based." (at 459)
  Rule:
    "We hold that contention is without merit." (at 459)
  Reasoning:
    In a footnote: "Under ch. 73—330, s 2 (appearing as s 83.47, Fla.Stat., F.S.A.) the exculpatory provision of this lease would be void. However, by a subsection thereof it would appear the statute is to be applied prospectively." (at 459)
  Question: Does the lease's exculpatory clause relieve the landlord or its agent of liability for an intentional tort?
  The issue in the court's words: "Secondly, appellant contends the exculpatory clause would not relieve the landlord or his agent from liability for an intentional tort." (at 459)
  Rule:
    "The exculpatory clause in the lease involved here will not exempt the agent from liability for his intentional tort, nor can it exempt the landlord from its liability therefor, under the doctrine of respondeat superior." (at 460)
    "While the exculpatory clause would be effective to bar a count based on Negligence, it would not so operate with respect to the claims asserted in the complaint based on allegations of intentional tort." (at 460)
  Reasoning:
    "However, in upholding the validity of said clauses the courts have recognized that they are not looked upon with favor and are enforceable only where and to the extent that such intention was made clear and unequivocal in the contract." (at 459)
    "Certainly the broad form exculpatory clause in the case sub judice would apprise the appellant herein that he has waived the landlord’s liability for injuries sustained in the normal use of the leasehold, but that language could not reasonably be construed to constitute an intelligent waiver of liability for an intentional tort committed upon the person of the appellant by the landlord and/or his agent." (at 460)
    "Furthermore, even if the exculpatory clause herein were capable of being construed to apply to an intentional tort, such an attempt to exempt one from liability for an intentional tort is generally declared void." (at 460)

A rent credit is not an option the tool tracks on its own. The closest part II rule applies when a failure is "due to causes beyond the control of the landlord and the landlord has made and continues to make every reasonable effort to correct the failure to comply" (Fla. Then, if the unit stays livable and you stay in it, "the rent for the period of noncompliance shall be reduced by an amount in proportion to the loss of rental value caused by the noncompliance" (Fla. Whether that is open to you is not known yet. It turns on these points:

- Whether the Wi-Fi counts. Your lease says rent includes "high-speed internet (Wi-Fi)" (Lease Section 5.1), and 83.56(1) reaches "material provisions of the rental agreement" (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)). The statute does not settle what "material" means. On one reading it turns on degree: how serious the failure is, how long it has lasted, and how it affects living in the unit. On the other, materiality comes from a duty 83.51(1) lists, and you confirmed this problem is not the structure or plumbing.
- The cause. Your lease says the landlord is not liable for outages "caused by a provider" (Lease Section 5.2). You confirmed the landlord only answers "the provider is aware". Whether the drops are beyond the landlord's control, and whether it keeps making every reasonable effort, the tool does not know.
- The rent you paid. The statute does not settle whether paying rent "with actual knowledge of a noncompliance by the landlord" (Fla. Stat. 83.56(5)(a)) waived this. If the nightly drops are a continuing noncompliance, paying does not waive it. On the other reading, paying with that knowledge waived it, and only a new or different failure starts over.

On acting together: it is not just you, but each path has limits.

Retaliation. A landlord may not retaliate by raising rent discriminatorily, cutting services, or bringing a possession or other civil action, where the tenant "has organized, encouraged, or participated in a tenant organization" (Fla. Stat. 83.64(1)(b)). The tenant "must have acted in good faith" (Fla. Stat. 83.64(1)). The statute text does not say whether a petition signed by about 40 tenants is a tenant organization, and you said it in chat but have not confirmed it on the sheet. The protection also "does not apply" where the landlord proves good cause, and good cause includes "good faith actions for nonpayment of rent" (Fla. Stat. 83.64(3)). Salmonte v. Eilertson, 526 So. 2d 179, read 83.64(3) and held the retaliation defense could not be raised by a tenant who admitted breaching the lease, because good cause included "violation of the rental agreement" (at 180). That tenant admitted unauthorized pets and other violations, and nothing you confirmed is a breach, so the case does not match you. It would cut against any tenant in your group who breaches the lease.

A joint suit. Whether tenants can sue together as a class is not an option the tool tracks, and whether a court would allow it is not settled. K.D. Lewis Enterprises Corp. v. Smith, 445 So. 2d 1032, read the 1981 statute. Tenants who complained the landlord did not maintain their apartments asked to represent all tenants in the complex, and the court held the trial court "correctly refused to permit the tenants to appear as representatives of a class" (at 1034), because the effect "would unquestionably vary from apartment to apartment and from tenant to tenant" (at 1034). That cuts against you where your losses differ, as the gym, study rooms, and Wi-Fi may from one tenant to the next. A later case, First Hanover v. Vazquez, follows it. Olen Properties Corp. v.
