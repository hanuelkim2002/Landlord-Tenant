# Playbooks become skills (design, 2026-10-12)

A dated design note, not kept current: the plan the author approved on 2026-10-12, which TODO items 109 to 116 carry
out. DOCUMENTATION.md says how the code works once each step is done.

## Why

After trying the web page on 2026-10-11 with a real lease, the author found the tool too rigid: intake was slow; the
sheet asked what the lease answers or the story made irrelevant (whether the tenant is a servicemember, whether the
landlord failed a repair, for a roommate's noise); after the sheet the tool waited instead of helping; answers listed
statute conditions and sent the tenant to "check your lease" though the tool holds the lease; and replies read as
separate answers, not a conversation. A code map of 2026-10-12 placed every line: the code that hands a job to the model
is 320 lines (7%); most of the rest exists to steer, check, or replace the model. The options layer (409 lines of code,
968 lines of playbook YAML) and the fact lists and sheet are what made it rigid.

## Decisions (the author's, 2026-10-12)

1. The YAML playbooks become skills: instructions the model reads, one markdown file per situation in `skills/`.
2. The model picks skills itself: the prompt lists every skill's name and one-line description, and a `load_skill`
   tool loads one, as Claude Code loads skills. This replaces the issue classifier, the yes/no issue rows on the
   sheet, the related-issue rules, and the stop page.
3. Only three things stay typed in a skill, in its header, because code needs them: the statute sections it loads,
   the deadline clocks (with the facts they count from), and the author's sign-off. Everything else is prose.
4. An answer may use what the tenant said in the chat, as "you told me", before it is confirmed; the record, the
   deadlines, and letters use confirmed facts only (README, non-negotiables).
5. The web page is the only front end: the terminal loop (`run.py`) goes.
6. The lease profile shrinks to what letters need; the model reads the rest of the lease with `search_lease`.

## What stays (README's promises)

- Dates: `compute_deadlines` is the only date arithmetic, from confirmed facts, with court calendars.
- Quotes and cites: every quote in a reply, a skill, or a letter is checked against its source; cites checked; the
  program prints the cited words under each reply.
- The record: `record.confirm_fact` is the only door into `matter["facts"]`; `events.jsonl` append-only; transcripts.
- Never sending; the read-only corpora (`statutes/`, `cases/`, `calendars/`); cases shown only after the author verifies
  their wiki pages.

## The skill format

`skills/<name>.md`, where `<name>` is the old playbook's issue label (`repair_not_made`). YAML front matter, then the
instructions.

```
---
name: repair_not_made
description: Something in the unit needs repair and the landlord has not fixed it.
sections: ["83.51", "83.56", "83.60", "83.64", "83.42"]
clocks:                         # corpus.Clock, unchanged: id, actor, trigger, days, direction, cite, quote,
  - id: ...                     # consequence, applies_if, count, shown_with, condition
counting:                       # convention: printed with every computed date; status and note: for people
  convention: ...
provenance:                     # statute_year, verified_against, verified_by, verified_on, version
  verified_by: null
---
## When to load this skill
## What to find out
## What the tenant can do
## Deadlines                    (only when the skill has clocks)
## What the statute does not settle
## Letters
## Mistakes to avoid
```

- `description` is one line naming the event that starts the situation (as the playbooks' descriptions do); it is how
  the model picks the skill.
- The facts a clock needs are typed by their role, with no separate list: `trigger` and `shown_with` are dates,
  `applies_if` is yes/no, and a court-days clock needs `premises_county` (text). These are the skill's clock facts.
- The body is written from the playbook's own verified content only: its guidance, its options' conditions and the
  statute words they quote, its open questions with their readings, its lease checks, exclusions, and failure modes.
  No statutory text, section number, or deadline from memory (CLAUDE.md guardrails).
- "What to find out" says what the answer turns on and where each comes from: the lease first (`search_lease`), then
  the tenant, asked what they saw, did, or hold, never whether something meets a term of the statute or the lease (the
  convention of 2026-10-08).
- No ids in the prose: the model copied them into replies. The prompt's facts part names the clock facts by id.

Checks (`skills.check_skill`, run by `smoke.py`, like the playbook checks before them): the header is valid (name
matches the file, description one line, every section in `statutes/`, every clock valid with its cite among the
sections and its quote word for word in that subsection, a counting convention when there are clocks); the required
headings are present, in order; every quotation of 20 characters or more in the body is word for word in a stored
section, and in the cited section and subsection when a cite is next to it; no clock id or clock fact id in the prose.

## A visit, after the change

1. New matter: the tenant, the lease PDF (read into the short profile), and the description. The description is the
   tenant's first chat message: the model loads the skills that fit, proposes the facts it heard, and answers.
2. Existing matter: "Anything new?" An answer is logged as a `tenant_update` event and is the first message; nothing
   new, and the chat waits.
3. Each turn: the model replies, loading skills, searching the lease and the statute, proposing facts, getting
   deadlines and cases, drafting letters, recording messages. After a turn that proposed facts, a short confirmation
   (each proposal with the tenant's words: yes, a correction, or skip) and then the model replies right away to what
   was confirmed.

## Facts

- `matter["facts"]` stays: id to value, written only by `record.confirm_fact`.
- `propose_facts` takes `{id: {value, how, said}}`. A clock fact of a loaded skill is parsed by its type (date, yes/no,
  county). Any other id (lowercase words joined by underscores, at most 40 characters) is kept as plain text. `said`
  must be in what the tenant typed this session, as now. A value equal to the confirmed one is refused.
- The confirmation asks only the proposals (and a changed fact: was this, now this?), never a missing fact.
- The prompt's facts part: confirmed facts, proposals waiting, and the clock facts each loaded skill still needs.

## Skills in the matter

- `matter["skills"]`: the loaded skill names, saved. `load_matter` turns an old matter's `issues` into `skills` (the
  names are the same) and drops `issues`, `proposed_issues`, `rejected_issues`.
- `load_skill(name)`: a name not in `skills/` is refused, with the names there are; an already loaded one says so;
  otherwise it is added, saved, and logged as a `skill_loaded` event. The skill's instructions and statute sections
  enter the system prompt (rebuilt on every call), so they hold for the rest of the matter and on a return visit.

## The prompt, after the change

Rules (rewritten: the opening, the format paragraph, and rules 1 to 4 as agreed with the author, rule 5 as suggested,
then rules 6 to 9 finished the same way: short, plain, a few examples at most), then: YOUR LEASE (the short profile, and
that `search_lease` reads the whole lease), SKILLS (each name and description, marked loaded), LOADED SKILLS (each loaded
skill's instructions), THE STATUTE (the loaded skills' sections), COURT CASES THIS TOOL HAS, YOUR FACTS, WHICH DEADLINES
HAVE PASSED. Gone: THE TOOL'S NOTES ON YOUR ISSUES and WHAT THE STATUTE DOES NOT SETTLE (both now inside each skill),
YOUR OPTIONS, and WHAT THE TOOL DOES NOT KNOW. A new rule: load each skill that fits before answering; if none fits,
say the tool has no skill for that situation and answer only from what `search_statute` finds.

## What goes

The issue classifier and `fill_facts` (the intake calls), issue suggestions and confirmation, related-issue rules, the
stop page and its lease word map, the playbooks and their loader and checks, the fact lists and fact status, the sheet's
missing-fact rows, the options layer (`check_options` and its ways, gates, and steps; the options and unknowns prompt
parts), the lease-versus-tenant comparison, the chat commands, the terminal (`run.py`, the terminal chat loop and
sheet), and the lease profile's fields letters do not use.

## The rules agreed so far

From the review of 2026-10-11 and 12, paused at rule 5. Step 114 adapts them to the skills (rule 1's "the options
part" becomes the loaded skill) and finishes rules 6 to 9 in the same style.

### Opening (agreed)

You help a Florida college tenant with a dispute with their landlord (Fla. Stat. ch. 83, part II). Find out what they want (to move out, get a repair, get the deposit back, answer a notice) and help them get there: read their lease and the statute, apply them to what they have told you, say whether they can do it and what is left to do, and help them keep a record showing they followed the law. Talk with them as in a conversation: build on what was already said instead of starting over. The tenant sends everything; you send nothing. The program works out every date; you never do. The program adds the line saying this is the tool's reading, not a lawyer's advice, so do not add your own.

### Format (agreed)

Write short, plain sentences in everyday words; when you need a legal term (possession, material, waive), say what it means. Cite, do not recite: give each rule in a plain sentence with its source in parentheses, written as (Fla. Stat. 83.56(1)) or as the lease clause's cite is given to you, (Lease Section 4.3) or (Lease, page 1); the program prints the cited words under your reply, so never paste a section or a lease clause into it, and quote only the few words a point turns on. Refer to what the tenant told you when it matters, naturally ("since your roommate is on the lease"), without listing it back. Say where a point comes from in plain words: "your lease says", "you told me", "the statute says". Never show the tool's insides: no ids like deposit_not_returned, no names of this prompt's parts, no rule numbers.

### Rule 1 (agreed)

1. Help the tenant do what they want.
Answer what they asked, first, in a natural reply that fits the question: a short question gets a short answer, and a follow-up builds on what you already said instead of starting over.
When they ask whether or how they can do something, the answer makes clear whether they can (the option's status as the options part gives it) before any step toward it, what that turns on, and what is left to do. Say each condition in a plain sentence with its cite, and whether their lease and what they told you meet it. When a condition turns on the lease, read the lease (search_lease when the clause is not given to you) and say what it says. When it turns on something you do not know, ask them. Send them to check only what you cannot read: their military orders, a local housing code, a document you do not have. A point the law does not settle: say so briefly, and what it turns on (rule 5).
When they are ready to act, you can offer to help keep a record that each step was done.
If they have not said what they want, name their options briefly and ask which they want. Never choose for them.
Use what the tenant has told you in this chat as "you told me", and propose it for their record (rule 4).
Every rule you rely on needs a cite whose words say it; a rule you cannot cite stays out. Never assume a fact you have not been given. When the lease and the statute differ, cite both, and say which controls only from a stored section (83.47). For a section not in the statute part, such as 83.55 on damages or 83.48 on attorney fees, use search_statute or get_statute_section.

### Rule 2 (agreed)

2. Cite and quote only what you were given: a section in the statute part or one you fetched (get_statute_section; search_statute finds a phrase), a lease clause given to you or found with search_lease, a case get_cases gave. Cite the subsection the words sit in. Words in quote marks are word for word from those or from the tenant, never from memory.
Before you say the lease does or does not say something, search it with search_lease, and try a second wording when the first finds nothing ("holdover", then "month-to-month"). Only when no wording finds it, say the tool found nothing on that in the lease, then what the statute says.
If nothing you have or can fetch answers the question, say so plainly and do not guess. First check whether an open question or a case topic is on the point, even in other words than the tenant's; if one is, give it (rules 5 and 8).

### Rule 3 (agreed)

3. Never work out a date. Dates come only from the tenant's facts, the lease, the tenant's words, or a tool's result; never count, add, or compare dates yourself, not even for "what if it ran from another day". When the day a period counts from is not settled, say the program counted from the date confirmed and counts nothing from any other.
Whether a deadline has passed comes only from the deadlines part ("passed" or "upcoming"). For any question about when, how long, or a last day, call get_deadlines: the program prints the dates above your reply, and you never write them. Call it whenever the tenant asks for deadlines; in passing, you can refer to deadlines already shown ("the deadline above") instead. When they asked only for the deadlines, a line is enough.

### Rule 4 (agreed)

4. When the tenant tells you a fact their issues need, call propose_facts with their exact words as "said"; the program checks the words, and the sheet asks them to confirm it after your reply. Until then it is what they told you, not a confirmed fact. Ask in the chat only for what your answer turns on, one or two things at a time, and never for something the facts part or the lease already holds.

### Rule 5 (suggested, not yet agreed when the review paused)

5. An open question in the way of what the tenant asked stays open: say in plain words that the law does not settle it, give each reading in a sentence with what it would mean for them, and never pick one or ask them to settle it. When one step works under every reading, say so. An open question with no readings is a limit of the tool: say what the tool does not work out.
