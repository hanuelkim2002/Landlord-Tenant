# Landlord's notice of a dangerous condition

Cases on what a person hurt on rental premises must show about the landlord's actual or constructive
knowledge of the condition that caused the injury. Both cases read 83.51(2)(a)3., on common areas, in
other issues; neither reads a part II provision on notice. The tenant's written notice under part II is a
separate topic: [[Tenant's notice of the landlord's noncompliance]].

## Cases

- [[Bennett v. Mattison]], Fla. 1st DCA 1980, reading the 1977 statute: Did the tenant need proof of how or when the water came to be on the hallway floor to reach the jury?
- [[Haynes v. Lloyd]], Fla. 5th DCA 1988, no statute year stated: Was there evidence from which a jury could infer that the landlord had constructive knowledge of the broken glass?

## How they relate

- Bennett, a fall on water in a common hallway, where the trial court had directed a verdict for want of proof of how or when the water got there: "This evidence was not essential to appellant’s recovery." ([[Bennett v. Mattison]] at 875) It points to warnings about a recurring condition: "The repeated verbal and written warnings to the owners that the hallway was slippery and dangerous put them on notice of the recurring nature of the problem and created a jury question as to whether the landlords exercised reasonable care to guard against a foreseeable danger." ([[Bennett v. Mattison]] at 875)
- Haynes, a child cut on broken glass under a tree, eight years later in another district; it does not cite Bennett: "A plaintiff asserting a cause of action based on premises liability must, among other things, plead and prove that the defendant possessor had actual knowledge of the dangerous condition before it caused the injury in question, or the plaintiff must plead and prove facts from which the trier of fact can reasonably infer constructive knowledge by the possessor of the dangerous operation, activity, or condition on the premises." ([[Haynes v. Lloyd]] at 946) On its record: "there was absolutely no evidence as to the period of time that the broken glass had been under the tree before the child fell." ([[Haynes v. Lloyd]] at 946)
- Cleanup and inspection. Bennett lists the theories the jury could hear: "However, appellants were entitled to submit the case to the jury under the theory that (1) appellees failed to take reasonable care to prevent water from coming on the floor; (2) appellees failed to take reasonable care by not having an inspection procedure to discover water on the floor and to clean it up; or (3) appellees failed to take reasonable care by not making the floor less slippery when water came to be on it." ([[Bennett v. Mattison]] at 875) In Haynes the landlord had a daily pickup: "The landlord testified on deposition that he had an employee named Hezikiah, who picked up around the apartments every morning, five days a week." ([[Haynes v. Lloyd]] at 947) The Haynes dissent: "As Hezikiah was to pickup in the morning, there is a question as to whether the landlord should have known (i.e., had notice) that its duty had not been properly executed." ([[Haynes v. Lloyd]] at 947)
- Notice from the tenant, in case pages not listed on this topic. [[Mansur v. Eubanks]]: "After the tenant takes possession, the landlord has a continuing duty to exercise reasonable care to repair dangerous defective conditions upon notice of their existence by the tenant, unless waived by the tenant." ([[Mansur v. Eubanks]] at 1330) [[Torres v. Garcia]], relying on Mansur: "The tenant did not consider this to be a problem and never complained to the landlord or asked the landlord to take any corrective action." ([[Torres v. Garcia]] at 95) The dissent in [[Lockamy v. Byrne]]: "I submit that there is no requirement that the tenant give notice of any allegedly defective condition of the premises, if the landlord had actual knowledge of the condition." ([[Lockamy v. Byrne]] at 373)
- Knowledge for a sufficient time, also in case pages not listed here. The Lockamy dissent quotes [[Paterson v. Deeb]]: "As we recognized in Paterson v. Deeb, 472 So.2d 1210, 1217 (Fla. 1st DCA 1985), “To state a cause of action in negligence, it should be alleged and proved that the landlord had actual or constructive knowledge of the violation said to be creating a dangerous condition for sufficient time to have it remedied." ([[Lockamy v. Byrne]] at 373) [[Smith v. Grove Apartments, LLC]], in a footnote: "In order to state a cause of action against a landlord for negligent failure to repair a dangerous or defective condition, the tenant need only allege that the landlord had either actual or constructive knowledge of the condition or of a statutory code violation for a sufficient time in which to make a correction." ([[Smith v. Grove Apartments, LLC]] at 584)
