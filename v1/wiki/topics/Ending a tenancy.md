# Ending a tenancy

Cases on how a residential tenancy comes to an end: the written notices part II sets out, the length of a
tenancy with no agreed term, and whether a tenant gave up the premises. Both cases listed are criminal
appeals that read these sections; see also [[Landlord and tenant law in criminal cases]].

Today's stored text:
- "A tenancy without a specific duration, as defined in s. 83.46 (2) or (3), may be terminated by either party giving written notice in the manner provided in s. 83.56 (4), as follows:" (Fla. Stat. 83.57)
- "If the rental agreement contains no provision as to duration of the tenancy, the duration is determined by the periods for which the rent is payable." (Fla. Stat. 83.46(2))
- "The landlord shall not recover possession of a dwelling unit except:" (Fla. Stat. 83.59(3))

## Cases

- [[Morse v. State]], Fla. 1st DCA 1992, reading the 1989 statute: Did Frank's 30-minute verbal order to leave end the motel tenancy, so that the deputy could rely on his invitation to search the room? Had the tenant abandoned the motel room before the search?
- [[Wolf v. State]], Fla. 2d DCA 2013, reading the 2009 statute: Did the trial court abuse its discretion by refusing a special jury instruction that recited parts of ch. 83 on ending a tenancy?

## How they relate

- A verbal order to leave. Morse, on a motel manager's 30-minute warning: "The manager of the Destination Motel could not terminate appellant’s tenancy on the spot by an immediate “verbal eviction,” for neither creating a disturbance nor being behind in rental payments invests the landlord with the legal right to terminate a tenancy without prior written notice." ([[Morse v. State]] at 500) Wolf does not cite Morse, but the special instruction Wolf requested opened with similar words: "A landlord cannot terminate a tenancy on the spot by an immediate “verbal eviction.”" ([[Wolf v. State]] at 1205) The Wolf majority's first reason for refusing it: "First, it is self-evident that the special instruction would have been confusing to the jury." ([[Wolf v. State]] at 1207) The Wolf dissent: "Schmid did not give Wolf the statutorily required notice that he was terminating the lease or file a complaint for possession; therefore he did not legally terminate the rental agreement with Wolf." ([[Wolf v. State]] at 1212)
- Length of the tenancy and the notice period. Morse read the 1989 texts of 83.46(2) and 83.57: "If appellant and the motel manager did not agree to a specific length of tenancy, then under section 83.46(2), Florida Statutes (1989), the periods for which rent was payable determined the duration of tenancy. Since appellant paid rent semi-monthly, his tenancy was for two-week periods." ([[Morse v. State]] at 500) And: "Even the shorter week-to-week tenancy requires no fewer than seven days’ written notice under section 83.57, Florida Statutes (1989), so it is clear appellant’s tenancy was not terminated by Frank’s 30–minute verbal warning." ([[Morse v. State]] at 500) The Wolf dissent, on a month-to-month tenancy, quoted 83.57(3) as "[fifteen] days’ notice prior to the end of any monthly period." ([[Wolf v. State]] at 1210) Today's text gives 30 days: "When the tenancy is from month to month, by giving not less than 30 days’ notice prior to the end of any monthly period" (Fla. Stat. 83.57(3)) Wolf states no year for the ch. 83 sections in the instruction; the 2009 year in its Cases line is the year it gives for the burglary statute.
- Notice for a breach. Morse read the 1989 texts of 83.56(2)(a) and 83.56(3): "Section 83.56(2)(a), Florida Statutes (1989), authorizes a landlord to terminate a tenancy for “continued unreasonable disturbance” by giving seven days’ written notice. Section 83.56(3), Florida Statutes (1989), makes nonpayment of rent the basis for termination, but only after three days expire, following delivery of the required written notice." ([[Morse v. State]] at 500) The three-day notice in civil cases: [[Three-day notice for nonpayment of rent]].
- Abandonment. Morse: "We find no intent on appellant’s part to relinquish his control over the room prior to removing his personal items such as clothing and furnishings." ([[Morse v. State]] at 502) The Wolf dissent: "I do recognize that a tenant can abandon or waive his possessory interest rights by his actions or words." ([[Wolf v. State]] at 1212) Neither case cites 83.59, whose stored text speaks of abandonment in its own words: "In the absence of actual knowledge of abandonment, it shall be presumed that the tenant has abandoned the dwelling unit if he or she is absent from the premises for a period of time equal to one-half the time for periodic rental payments." (Fla. Stat. 83.59(3)(c))
