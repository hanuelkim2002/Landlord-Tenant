# Interpreting the lease

Cases on how a court reads the terms of a lease: clauses that pull against each other, typed terms against
printed ones, plain meaning and a merger clause, reforming a lease for mutual mistake, and a clause on waiver.
None of them reads part II. Berwick, on a motel lease, predates the 1973 Act; Kaplan, on a warehouse leased
for a bingo hall, reads 83.20(2) in part I (nonresidential); Ralston (business tenants in a hotel building),
Malt (a warehouse), and Philpot (a condominium unit with an option to buy) cite no section of ch. 83.

## Cases

- [[Berwick Corp. v. Kleinginna Investment Corp.]], Fla. 3d DCA 1962, no statute year stated: Did the lease clause putting the risk of water damage on the lessee bar the claim of constructive eviction?
- [[Ralston, Inc. v. Miller]], Fla. 3d DCA 1978, no statute year stated: Did the lease's printed clauses on water damage relieve the lessor of the typed covenant to keep the roof and structure in repair?
- [[Malt v. R.J. Mueller Enterprises]], Fla. 4th DCA 1981, no statute year stated: Was the lease properly reformed on the ground of mutual mistake about the size of the building?
- [[Philpot v. Bouchelle]], Fla. 1st DCA 1982, no statute year stated: Did the lessor waive the conditions of the option by accepting late rent and the improvements, given the lease's non-waiver clause?
- [[Kaplan v. McCabe]], Fla. 5th DCA 1988, reading the 1985 statute: When did the lease term begin under its certificate-of-occupancy provision? Was an earlier July lease properly kept out of evidence?

## How they relate

- Ralston follows Berwick on a water-damage clause and quotes it. Berwick, in 1962: "In construing a lease, as in other contracts, it is necessary for the court to gather the meaning of the parties from the whole instrument and not from a few lines taken here and there." ([[Berwick Corp. v. Kleinginna Investment Corp.]] at 688) Ralston, in 1978: "As in the matter before this court, the landlord in Berwick sought to avoid damages by the defense of a provision of the lease that the lessee was required to take the risk of damage to the property by reason of water, bursting pipes, gas leaks, water system or sewerage leaking, etc." ([[Ralston, Inc. v. Miller]] at 1070) It then quotes Berwick's reading: "This provision of the lease cannot be construed to destroy the effect of the requirement that the landlord keep the roof sound and watertight." ([[Ralston, Inc. v. Miller]] at 1070)
- Clauses that pull against each other. Berwick: "Further, it is the duty of the court whereever possible to construe apparently conflicting provisions of a contract so that the provisions do not conflict." ([[Berwick Corp. v. Kleinginna Investment Corp.]] at 688) Ralston also relies on a rule for typed against printed terms, quoting Allegheny Mutual Casualty Co. v. State, not in `cases/`: "There is the rule, applicable and directly controlling here, that where a part of a contract is written or typed and part is printed, and the written or typed and the printed part are apparently inconsistent or there is reasonable doubt as to the sense and meaning of the total instrument, the words in writing or typing will control." ([[Ralston, Inc. v. Miller]] at 1069) Both clauses are also on [[Exculpatory clauses in a lease]].
- Plain meaning. Ralston, on the typed paragraph: "Paragraph 23 of the lease, especially written and inserted in the lease by the parties, is clear and unambiguous." ([[Ralston, Inc. v. Miller]] at 1069) And on one word in it: "The word “may ”, when given its ordinary meaning, denotes a permissive term rather than the mandatory connotation of the word “shall”" ([[Ralston, Inc. v. Miller]] at 1070) Kaplan, on when the term began: "The trial court’s finding of fact concerning the effective date of the lease is clearly erroneous because it fails to follow the plain meaning of the lease agreement." ([[Kaplan v. McCabe]] at 1356)
- What the writing holds. Kaplan, on an earlier lease and a merger clause: "While not the main issue on appeal, the trial court was within its discretion in excluding a July lease inasmuch as the September lease agreement was not ambiguous and appeared on its face to be the entire agreement between the parties." ([[Kaplan v. McCabe]] at 1357) Malt, on changing the writing for a mistake both sides made: "A court will reform a contract if it fails to express the parties’ intentions because of fraud, mutual mistake, accident or inequitable conduct." ([[Malt v. R.J. Mueller Enterprises]] at 1176) and "Here, the tenant offered clear and convincing evidence of a mutual mistake, the size of the building, and the trial court properly reformed the contract." ([[Malt v. R.J. Mueller Enterprises]] at 1176) Kaplan does not cite Malt.
- A clause on waiver. Philpot reads the lease's non-waiver clause against the common law of waiver: "We consider that the parties contractually modified the common law rules of waiver and estoppel, and their modification does not conflict with any public policy." ([[Philpot v. Bouchelle]] at 1344) Without the clause, the court said: "If not for paragraph 15 of the parties’ contract, we would agree that the issue as to whether the appellant waived the conditions necessary to the exercise of the option was a jury question and that there was competent, substantial evidence supporting the jury’s decision." ([[Philpot v. Bouchelle]] at 1343) Malt, on a different question (whether to evict), also speaks of a landlord's taking late rent: "The landlord accepted late rent payments from the tenant and was also found to be in substantial breach of the sublease." ([[Malt v. R.J. Mueller Enterprises]] at 1176) Neither cites the other. Taking rent after a breach is the subject of [[Accepting or refusing rent]].
- Reading a lease together with another document. Wilson, a part II case listed on [[Liquidated damages and early termination fees]] and not here, on a form lease and an addendum signed with it, quotes Citicorp Real Estate, Inc. v. Ameripalms 6B GP, Inc., not in `cases/`: "The law is well established that two or more documents executed by the same parties, at or near the same time, and concerning the same transaction or subject matter are generally construed together as a single contract." ([[Wilson v. Terwillinger]] at 1124)
