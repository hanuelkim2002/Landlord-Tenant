# Landlord and tenant law in criminal cases

Criminal appeals in which a court reads part II to answer a question of criminal law: whether the police
could search a tenant's room at the landlord's invitation (Morse), and whether a burglary jury should have
been told the rules for ending a tenancy (Wolf). The part II sections they read are gathered under
[[Ending a tenancy]].

## Cases

- [[Morse v. State]], Fla. 1st DCA 1992, reading the 1989 statute: Did Frank's 30-minute verbal order to leave end the motel tenancy, so that the deputy could rely on his invitation to search the room? Had the tenant abandoned the motel room before the search? Could the deputy rely in good faith on Frank's claim of authority to let him into the room?
- [[Wolf v. State]], Fla. 2d DCA 2013, reading the 2009 statute: Did the trial court abuse its discretion by refusing a special jury instruction that recited parts of ch. 83 on ending a tenancy?

## How they relate

- Why part II mattered in each. Morse, on a search: "However, Florida law governing the duration and termination of a tenancy is relevant to the question whether appellant retained a legitimate privacy interest, for Fourth Amendment purposes, with respect to the remaining personal belongings in Room 11." ([[Morse v. State]] at 501) And: "However, because the purported eviction was invalid under Florida law, under these particular facts the police cannot rely on the good-faith exception to the exclusionary rule" ([[Morse v. State]] at 502) Wolf, on burglary, in the dissent: "That is, if he held a legal right to possess/occupy the property and if that legal right had not been terminated in the statutorily prescribed manner, then Wolf was licensed to enter under section 810.02(1)(b)(1) and could present a defense that he did not commit burglary." ([[Wolf v. State]] at 1213)
- Wolf does not cite Morse, though the special instruction Wolf requested opened with words close to Morse's. Morse: "The manager of the Destination Motel could not terminate appellant’s tenancy on the spot by an immediate “verbal eviction,” for neither creating a disturbance nor being behind in rental payments invests the landlord with the legal right to terminate a tenancy without prior written notice." ([[Morse v. State]] at 500) The instruction: "A landlord cannot terminate a tenancy on the spot by an immediate “verbal eviction.”" ([[Wolf v. State]] at 1205)
- The three Wolf opinions. The majority, on the defense's choices at trial: "Thus, whatever defense Mr. Wolf thought the “unlawful eviction” provided, he apparently did not believe it meant he was licensed to enter the home." ([[Wolf v. State]] at 1208) Judge Peters, concurring specially: "Arguably, a special instruction containing specific, applicable provisions of the Florida Residential Landlord and Tenant Act together with such other principles of law as may be required to make any such special instruction both accurate and complete could have been given to augment the standard instruction. However, no such instruction was presented or requested." ([[Wolf v. State]] at 1208) Chief Judge Davis, dissenting: "And Schmid’s attempt to simply assert actual physical control over the premises does not, in and of itself, terminate the lease agreement or the rights conveyed thereunder." ([[Wolf v. State]] at 1212)
