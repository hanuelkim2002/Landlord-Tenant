# Landlord's liability for injuries on the premises

Cases on a residential landlord's liability in negligence when a tenant, or someone with the tenant, is hurt
by a condition of the rented premises: the duty before and after the tenant takes possession, the tenant's
own knowledge of the condition, and the part 83.51 or a housing code plays in the claim. Torres cites no
section of ch. 83 and reads Mansur instead.

Today's stored text:
- "The landlord at all times during the tenancy shall: (a) Comply with the requirements of applicable building, housing, and health codes; or (b) Where there are no applicable building, housing, or health codes, maintain the roofs, windows, doors, floors, steps, porches, exterior walls, foundations, and all other structural components in good repair" (Fla. Stat. 83.51(1))
- "Unless otherwise agreed in writing, in addition to the requirements of subsection (1), the landlord of a dwelling unit other than a single-family home or duplex shall, at all times during the tenancy, make reasonable provisions for: ... 3. The clean and safe condition of common areas." (Fla. Stat. 83.51(2)(a))
- "The landlord is not responsible to the tenant under this section for conditions created or caused by the negligent or wrongful act or omission of the tenant, a member of the tenant’s family, or other person on the premises with the tenant’s consent." (Fla. Stat. 83.51(4))

## Cases

- [[Bennett v. Mattison]], Fla. 1st DCA 1980, reading the 1977 statute: Did the tenant need proof of how or when the water came to be on the hallway floor to reach the jury? Did the tenant's alleged superior knowledge of the slippery hallway bar her recovery?
- [[Mansur v. Eubanks]], Fla. 1981, reading the 1977 statute: Does the landlord of a residential dwelling unit owe the tenant a duty to inspect and repair before the tenant takes possession, or does the rule of caveat lessee apply? What duty does the landlord owe after the tenant takes possession? Were the facts on possession and on the cause of the explosion in dispute, so as to stand in the way of summary judgment?
- [[Lockamy v. Byrne]], Fla. 1st DCA 1985, reading the 1973 and 1977 statutes: Was summary judgment for the landlords correct on the tenants' claim for injuries from the fall on the steps?
- [[Bennett M. Lifter, Inc. v. Varnado]], Fla. 3d DCA 1985, reading the 1983 statute: Did the county housing code impose a duty on the landlord to provide hot water, and was its violation properly put to the jury as evidence of negligence? Does Standard Civil Jury Instruction 3.5(i), on a landlord's failure to repair a dangerous or defective condition after notice, depart from Mansur v. Eubanks?
- [[Haynes v. Lloyd]], Fla. 5th DCA 1988, no statute year stated: Was broken glass on the ground where young children play a dangerous condition? Was there evidence from which a jury could infer that the landlord had constructive knowledge of the broken glass?
- [[Ray v. Tampa Windridge Associates]], Fla. 2d DCA 1991, reading the 1985 statute: Are the release's provisions on the tenant's non-reliance, and on the landlord's not checking or guaranteeing the locks, prohibited by 83.47?
- [[Torres v. Garcia]], Fla. 3d DCA 1997, no statute year stated: Was the landlord entitled to a directed verdict when the tenant never told the landlord of the pressed-down carpet? Was there a difference in level between the tile and the carpet when they were installed?
- [[Charlemagne v. Francis]], Fla. 4th DCA 1997, reading the 1993 statute: Was it error to instruct the jury on section 83.51(4) in a suit brought only in common law negligence?
- [[Menendez v. Palms West Condo. Ass'n]], Fla. 1st DCA 1999, no statute year stated: Was the absence of a doorscope a defect or dangerous condition that the landlord had a duty to correct?
- [[Smith v. Grove Apartments, LLC]], Fla. 3d DCA 2007, reading the 2007 statute: Was it for the trier of fact to decide whether the tenant's injury, suffered while trimming the trees himself after the landlord allegedly refused, was a foreseeable result of the landlord's alleged failure to maintain the parking lot? Did the landlord owe the tenant a statutory duty to maintain the parking lot, and was the tenant within the class the statute protects?

## How they relate

- Mansur, the Supreme Court's 1981 decision, speaks to two periods. Before possession: "We hold that the owner of a residential dwelling unit, who leases it to a tenant for residential purposes, has a duty to reasonably inspect the premises before allowing the tenant to take possession, and to make the repairs necessary to transfer a reasonably safe dwelling unit to the tenant unless defects are waived by the tenant." ([[Mansur v. Eubanks]] at 1329) After possession: "After the tenant takes possession, the landlord has a continuing duty to exercise reasonable care to repair dangerous defective conditions upon notice of their existence by the tenant, unless waived by the tenant." ([[Mansur v. Eubanks]] at 1330) The Lockamy dissent quotes the first; Torres and Charlemagne quote the second, and Torres rests on it: "Assuming that the pressed down condition of the carpet could be said to constitute a dangerous condition, the landlord was entitled to a directed verdict on authority of Mansur v. Eubanks, 401 So.2d 1328 (Fla.1981)." ([[Torres v. Garcia]] at 95)
- Before and after Mansur, one judge. Judge Ervin, specially concurring in Bennett in 1980, read the statute against the older common law rule: "In my judgment the statute, to that limited extent, removes the common law rule absolving the landlord from any duty to his tenants for injuries caused to them by defects on the leased premises, unless his knowledge of the defect is superior to that of the tenant." ([[Bennett v. Mattison]] at 875) He cited Brooks v. Peters for that rule; Mansur overruled it: "We resolve the conflict by overruling Brooks v. Peters and adopting the Alexander rule." ([[Mansur v. Eubanks]] at 1329) Mansur points to his concurrence in the decision it reviewed: "This latter duty corresponds to the statutory warranty of habitability noted by Judge Ervin in his concurring opinion below." ([[Mansur v. Eubanks]] at 1330) Judge Ervin also wrote the Lockamy dissent.
- The tenant's own knowledge of the condition. Bennett: "Second, Melena’s knowledge, if any, would present an issue of comparative negligence and would not bar her recovery." ([[Bennett v. Mattison]] at 875) The Lockamy dissent: "Additionally, after the passage of the Florida Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, Sections 83.40-.66, Florida Statutes (1973), the fact that the tenant’s knowledge is equal to that of the landlord’s can no longer be considered a bar to an action for damages." ([[Lockamy v. Byrne]] at 373) The Lockamy majority gave no reasons. Menendez, which cites neither, on a door without a viewer: "Furthermore, the absence of the doorscope in the front door of the apartment was not a hidden or concealed condition. To the contrary, the physical features of the front door were as apparent to the tenant as they were to the landlord." ([[Menendez v. Palms West Condo. Ass'n]] at 61)
- Which conditions. Lifter, reading Mansur against the standard jury instruction: "While Mansur addressed a condition which was both defective and dangerous, it did not purport to exonerate the landlord from responsibility for faulty injury-producing conditions which are not inherently dangerous." ([[Bennett M. Lifter, Inc. v. Varnado]] at 1341) Menendez, citing Mansur and not Lifter: "This duty is limited, however, to conditions that are inherently unsafe or dangerous conditions that are not readily apparent to the tenant." ([[Menendez v. Palms West Condo. Ass'n]] at 61) Haynes, on broken glass: "we can, and do, rule as a matter of law that broken glass known to be on the ground where young children are known to play, constitutes a dangerous condition." ([[Haynes v. Lloyd]] at 945)
- A statute or code in a negligence claim. Bennett, on 83.51(2)(a)3.: "The violation of this statutory duty would be evidence of negligence." ([[Bennett v. Mattison]] at 875) Lifter, on a county housing code: "The court properly left the matter to the trier of fact when it instructed the jury that violation of the code could be considered as evidence of negligence." ([[Bennett M. Lifter, Inc. v. Varnado]] at 1338) Lifter on the protected class: "Although the question whether a plaintiff is within the class which the ordinance was intended to protect is a question of fact, it cannot be disputed here that plaintiffs, as tenants, were within the protected class." ([[Bennett M. Lifter, Inc. v. Varnado]] at 1338) Smith, which follows Lifter, in nearly the same words: "However, in this case, it cannot be disputed that Smith as a tenant, was within the protected class." ([[Smith v. Grove Apartments, LLC]] at 587) Charlemagne's suit was in common law negligence only: "Although, under that section, the landlord has the duty to keep the floors in good repair, the appellant did not sue for a violation of that duty, which imposes definite obligations upon a landlord that did not exist under the common law." ([[Charlemagne v. Francis]] at 160)
- Who was hurt. Not every plaintiff was the tenant. Haynes: "Appellant, a four and one-half year old child visiting a tenant at apartments owned by appellee-landlord, fell from a tree and cut his arm on a piece of broken glass that was on the ground." ([[Haynes v. Lloyd]] at 945) Charlemagne, in a footnote: "Both parties have treated this case as one between landlord and tenant, even though appellant does not appear to have been a tenant, but instead the tenant’s invitee." ([[Charlemagne v. Francis]] at 159) In Torres the plaintiff was the tenant's sister, and in Mansur an injured neighbor and an incoming tenant sued.
