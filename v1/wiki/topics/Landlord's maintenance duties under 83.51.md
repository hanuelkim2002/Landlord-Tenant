# Landlord's maintenance duties under 83.51

Cases on what 83.51 asks of a residential landlord during the tenancy, and on the part those duties play in
a tenant's suit: as evidence of negligence, as a warranty of habitability, and on locks and common areas.
Solomon cites no part of 83.51; it reads 83.42 and 83.43 and asks about an implied warranty of fitness.

Today's stored text:
- "The landlord at all times during the tenancy shall: (a) Comply with the requirements of applicable building, housing, and health codes; or (b) Where there are no applicable building, housing, or health codes, maintain the roofs, windows, doors, floors, steps, porches, exterior walls, foundations, and all other structural components in good repair and capable of resisting normal forces and loads and the plumbing in reasonable working condition." (Fla. Stat. 83.51(1))
- "Unless otherwise agreed in writing, in addition to the requirements of subsection (1), the landlord of a dwelling unit other than a single-family home or duplex shall, at all times during the tenancy, make reasonable provisions for: ... 2. Locks and keys. 3. The clean and safe condition of common areas. ... 5. Functioning facilities for heat during winter, running water, and hot water." (Fla. Stat. 83.51(2)(a))
- "The landlord’s obligations under this subsection may be altered or modified in writing with respect to a single-family home or duplex." (Fla. Stat. 83.51(1))

## Cases

- [[Bennett v. Mattison]], Fla. 1st DCA 1980, reading the 1977 statute: What bearing does 83.51(2)(a) 3 have on a negligence claim over a fall in a common hallway?
- [[Solomon v. Gentry]], Fla. 4th DCA 1980, reading the 1979 statute: May a landlord be liable to a tenant for breach of an implied warranty of fitness for a specific purpose?
- [[Mansur v. Eubanks]], Fla. 1981, reading the 1977 statute: What duty does the landlord owe after the tenant takes possession?
- [[Paterson v. Deeb]], Fla. 1st DCA 1985, reading the 1973 and 1981 statutes: Did the amended complaint state a cause of action in negligence for the attack, though it alleged no prior similar crime on the premises? Is a landlord's violation of 83.51 negligence per se, or evidence of negligence? Does the 83.51 duty to provide locks and keys reach the building's outside entrances and the hallway bathroom door?
- [[Bennett M. Lifter, Inc. v. Varnado]], Fla. 3d DCA 1985, reading the 1983 statute: Does Standard Civil Jury Instruction 3.5(i), on a landlord's failure to repair a dangerous or defective condition after notice, depart from Mansur v. Eubanks?
- [[Haynes v. Lloyd]], Fla. 5th DCA 1988, no statute year stated: What bearing does 83.51(2)(a)3., on the safe condition of common areas, have on the claim?
- [[Ray v. Tampa Windridge Associates]], Fla. 2d DCA 1991, reading the 1985 statute: Did the tenant's signed security release dispense with the landlord's duty under 83.51(2)(a) to make reasonable provisions for locks?
- [[Menendez v. Palms West Condo. Ass'n]], Fla. 1st DCA 1999, no statute year stated: Did the Residential Landlord and Tenant Act impose a duty to install a doorscope? Could the managing association be liable for breach of the lease, or for failing to keep the common areas safe under 83.51(2)(a)?
- [[Smith v. Grove Apartments, LLC]], Fla. 3d DCA 2007, reading the 2007 statute: Did the landlord owe the tenant a statutory duty to maintain the parking lot, and was the tenant within the class the statute protects?

## How they relate

- Evidence of negligence. Bennett, in 1980: "The violation of this statutory duty would be evidence of negligence." ([[Bennett v. Mattison]] at 875) Paterson cites Bennett: "A landlord’s violation of the act is at least evidence of negligence. Bennett v. Mattison, 382 So.2d 873 (Fla. 1st DCA 1980)" ([[Paterson v. Deeb]] at 1216) and adds, in a footnote: "We do not consider this section to be a “strict liability statute” in the sense that it protects a particular class of persons from their inability to protect themselves" ([[Paterson v. Deeb]] at 1217)
- The statute and the common law. Paterson: "It is clear that the Florida Residential and Landlord-Tenant Act imposes definite obligations upon the landlord which continue throughout the term of the lease and which did not exist under the common law." ([[Paterson v. Deeb]] at 1216) [[Charlemagne v. Francis]] cites Paterson in like words. Haynes, which cites neither, on the common areas clause: "because that statute merely restates the landlord’s common law duty as it would exist in the absence of a statute." ([[Haynes v. Lloyd]] at 945) The Haynes dissent: "The landlord therefore had a statutory duty to maintain the premises in a clean and safe condition." ([[Haynes v. Lloyd]] at 947) Bennett's special concurrence reads the clause as removing a common law rule in part: "In my judgment the statute, to that limited extent, removes the common law rule absolving the landlord from any duty to his tenants for injuries caused to them by defects on the leased premises, unless his knowledge of the defect is superior to that of the tenant." ([[Bennett v. Mattison]] at 875)
- Warranty language. Solomon, in 1980, on an implied warranty of fitness: "We hold that no such warranty arises under a lease of real property and reverse." ([[Solomon v. Gentry]] at 53) Mansur, the next year, which does not cite Solomon: "This latter duty corresponds to the statutory warranty of habitability noted by Judge Ervin in his concurring opinion below." ([[Mansur v. Eubanks]] at 1330) Paterson: "The act has been characterized as creating a “statutory warranty of habitability,” Mansur v. Eubanks, 401 So.2d 1328, 1330 (Fla.1981)." ([[Paterson v. Deeb]] at 1216) It leaves a claim on the implied warranty open: "We do not consider whether the facts alleged in the amended complaint are sufficient to state a cause of action for breach of implied warranty of habitability recognized by the Supreme Court in Mansur v. Eubanks, 401 So.2d 1328 (Fla.1981)." ([[Paterson v. Deeb]] at 1220) Lifter, on hot water: "The duty, noted the court, corresponds to the statutory warranty of habitability, found in section 83.51, Florida Statutes (1983), which requires a landlord to provide hot running water." ([[Bennett M. Lifter, Inc. v. Varnado]] at 1341) Today's text lists hot water in 83.51(2)(a), above.
- Locks and keys. Paterson: "Since the purpose of the statutory duty to provide locks is the prevention of criminal acts by unauthorized persons having unrestrained access to the premises, we conclude that the landlord’s duty to reasonably provide protection from criminal attack does not have to be implied from prior similar occurrences on the leased premises." ([[Paterson v. Deeb]] at 1217) Menendez distinguishes Paterson: "The duty in Paterson was based on a violation of an express provision of the statute. No such violation occurred here." ([[Menendez v. Palms West Condo. Ass'n]] at 62) and "Whether the apartment could be locked is immaterial, because the individuals at the door were allowed inside. In any event, the apartment was secured by an operable lock as required by statute." ([[Menendez v. Palms West Condo. Ass'n]] at 62) Ray, which cites neither, on a signed release: "even assuming that section 83.51(2)(a) permits a landlord-tenant agreement relieving the landlord of that duty, the Apartment Security Acknowledgement and Release document in this case does not do so but actually indicates that the landlord will make reasonable provisions for locks on the tenant’s apartment." ([[Ray v. Tampa Windridge Associates]] at 677)
- Common areas. Bennett, a hallway and patio: "Since both the hallway and patio were provided for the common use and benefit of the tenants, appellees were required by Section 83.51(2)(a) 3, F.S. (1977) to keep these areas safe and clean." ([[Bennett v. Mattison]] at 875) Smith, a parking lot: "The parking lot area in the instant case is most assuredly a common area of the leased premises." ([[Smith v. Grove Apartments, LLC]] at 586) The Smith dissent discusses Bennett: "In Bennett v. Mattison, 382 So.2d 873 (Fla. 1st DCA 1980), the court applied the statute to a slip and fall case reasoning that because the hallway and patio were provided for the common use and benefit of the tenants, the landlord was required by section 83.51(2)(a) 3 to keep these areas safe and clean." ([[Smith v. Grove Apartments, LLC]] at 592) Menendez, inside an apartment: "The attack on Julio Menendez, Jr. did not occur in a common area of the Palms West complex." ([[Menendez v. Palms West Condo. Ass'n]] at 62) Paterson, in a footnote, reads the term dwelling unit: "The term “dwelling unit” is defined in section 83.43(2) to mean a structure or part of a structure that is rented for use as a residence, and is broad enough to include the bathroom and common areas." ([[Paterson v. Deeb]] at 1217) Today's stored definition sits in 83.43(5)(a): "A structure or part of a structure that is rented for use as a home, residence, or sleeping place by one person or by two or more persons who maintain a common household." (Fla. Stat. 83.43(5)(a))
- The wording the courts read. Paterson quoted the 1981 text of (2)(a) in pieces: "landlord of a dwelling unit ... shall, at all times during the tenancy, make reasonable provision for ... locks and keys [and] the clean and safe condition of common areas." ([[Paterson v. Deeb]] at 1216) The Haynes dissent, with no year, and Smith, quoting the 2007 text for a fall in 2002, give the opening of (2)(a) in the words it has today: "Unless otherwise agreed in writing, in addition to the requirements of subsection (1), the landlord of a dwelling unit other than a single-family home or duplex shall, at all times during the tenancy, make reasonable provisions for ... 3. The clean and safe condition of common areas." ([[Haynes v. Lloyd]] at 947) The Lockamy dissent quoted (1)(b) with no year: "maintain the roofs, windows, screens, doors, floors, steps, porches, ... in good repair" ([[Lockamy v. Byrne]] at 373) Today's (1)(b), above, does not list screens among those items; it gives them sentences of their own: "The landlord, at commencement of the tenancy, must ensure that screens are installed in a reasonable condition. Thereafter, the landlord must repair damage to screens once annually, when necessary, until termination of the rental agreement." (Fla. Stat. 83.51(1)(b))
