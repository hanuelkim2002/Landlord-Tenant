# Accepting or refusing rent

Cases on what follows when a landlord accepts rent, or refuses a tenant's tender of it, after a breach:
waiver, estoppel, and a lease's non-waiver clause. None is a residential case under part II: Moskos was
decided in 1971, before the 1973 Act, and reads sections now in part I; Bodden (a laundry room at an apartment complex) and
Philpot (a condominium lease with an option to purchase) cite no section of ch. 83; and Multach concerns a
commercial lease of a retail store.

Today's stored text:
- "If the landlord accepts rent with actual knowledge of a noncompliance by the tenant or accepts performance by the tenant of any other provision of the rental agreement that is at variance with its provisions, or if the tenant pays rent with actual knowledge of a noncompliance by the landlord or accepts performance by the landlord of any other provision of the rental agreement that is at variance with its provisions, the landlord or tenant waives his or her right to terminate the rental agreement or to bring a civil action for that noncompliance, but not for any subsequent or continuing noncompliance." (Fla. Stat. 83.56(5)(a))
- "However, a landlord does not waive the right to terminate the rental agreement or to bring a civil action for that noncompliance by accepting partial rent for the period." (Fla. Stat. 83.56(5)(a))

## Cases

- [[Moskos v. Hand]], Fla. 4th DCA 1971, no statute year stated: Did the tenant's answer, saying the landlords accepted the next month's rent, raise waiver and estoppel issues that barred a judgment on the pleadings?
- [[Bodden v. Carbonell]], Fla. 2d DCA 1978, reading the 1975 statute: Could the new owner rely on the lease's lack of one witness under 689.01 as a defense?
- [[Philpot v. Bouchelle]], Fla. 1st DCA 1982, no statute year stated: Did the lessor waive the conditions of the option by accepting late rent and the improvements, given the lease's non-waiver clause?
- [[Multach v. Adams]], Fla. 4th DCA 1982, reading the 1981 statute: Did the landlord's refusal of the tenant's late tender of rent waive or discharge the rent owed? Did the landlord waive the lease terms on the alterations by accepting rent after them?

## How they relate

- Philpot cites Moskos on accepting rent after a breach. Moskos: "As a general rule a lessor is estopped to assert a forfeiture for a breach of a condition in a lease, or he waives his right to such a forfeiture, where after the breach he accepts rent from his tenant with knowledge or full notice thereof." ([[Moskos v. Hand]] at 796) Philpot: "The law is clear that a lessor is estopped to assert a forfeiture for a breach of covenant or condition in a lease, or waives his right to such a forfeiture, if, after the breach of covenant, he accepts rent from his tenant with knowledge or full notice thereof." ([[Philpot v. Bouchelle]] at 1344)
- Philpot turns to the lease's non-waiver clause: "We consider that the parties contractually modified the common law rules of waiver and estoppel, and their modification does not conflict with any public policy." ([[Philpot v. Bouchelle]] at 1344) And: "Thus, the lessor, by accepting appellee’s late performance, can hardly be considered as dealing inconsistently with the terms of the contract." ([[Philpot v. Bouchelle]] at 1344) Moskos, decided on the pleadings, mentions no such clause: "We believe the content of foregoing pleadings was of such nature as to make a justiciable issue in the areas of waiver and estoppel adequate to bar a judgment on the pleadings." ([[Moskos v. Hand]] at 796)
- A residential lease clause of this kind under 83.56(5) is a question Bentley, which is not listed on this topic, declined to answer: "We decline to answer the second certified question as to whether section 83.56(5), Florida Statutes (1983) precludes a landlord from inserting a provision in the lease which allows him to accept late or partial rent from a tenant without waiving his right to evict." ([[Investment & Income Realty, Inc. v. Bentley]] at 220) Today's text of 83.56(5)(a), above, speaks to partial rent in its own sentence.
- Refusing a tender. In Moskos, the landlords held the tenant's late check: "The defendant’s sworn answer reflected that he had paid and the landlords had accepted the next month’s rent (January and that he had tardily tendered the December rent in the form of a Cashier’s Check which the landlords held but refused to negotiate." ([[Moskos v. Hand]] at 796) Multach, on a refused tender: "We hold that the refusal of the tender did not constitute a waiver or a forgiveness of the debt." ([[Multach v. Adams]] at 1255) And: "However, the refusal to accept a proper tender will prevent the collection of interest or other damages because the failure to receive payment is due to the promisee’s own action." ([[Multach v. Adams]] at 1255) Both opinions are Judge Walden's, in the Fourth District; Multach does not cite Moskos.
- Multach and the statute. Multach quotes the 1981 text of 83.56(5): "If the landlord accepts rent with actual knowledge of a noncompliance by the tenant or accepts performance by the tenant of any other provision of the rental agreement that is at variance with its provisions, ... the landlord or tenant waives his right to terminate the rental agreement or to bring a civil action for that noncompliance, but not for any subsequent or continuing noncompliance." ([[Multach v. Adams]] at 1256) And, on its commercial lease: "However, this statute is applicable only to the rental of a dwelling unit and mobile home lot." ([[Multach v. Adams]] at 1256) Today's text, above, reads his or her where the 1981 text reads his, and goes on to sentences on partial rent that Multach does not quote.
- Bodden reads 689.01 (1975), outside ch. 83, on a five-year lease that lacked a witness; the 1975 year in its Cases line is that statute's. It cites none of the other cases here. On the new owner's acceptance of rent: "Although the lease does not comply with s 689.01, Fla.Stat. (1975) for lack of one witness to the five-year agreement, appellee is estopped by his conduct in accepting rental payments to assert this as a defense." ([[Bodden v. Carbonell]] at 929)
