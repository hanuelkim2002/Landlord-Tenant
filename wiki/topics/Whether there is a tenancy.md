# Whether there is a tenancy

Cases on whether an occupant held as a tenant under a rental agreement or in some other way: as a buyer
under a contract, a licensee, someone claiming an ownership interest, or the renter of a mobile home that
might be a chattel. Two cite no section of ch. 83: Bodden, a commercial agreement for a laundry room, and
Purcell, a contract for deed read under the mortgage statute.

Today's stored text:
- "“Tenant” means any person entitled to occupy a dwelling unit under a rental agreement." (Fla. Stat. 83.43(17))
- "“Rental agreement” means any written agreement, including amendments or addenda, or oral agreement for a duration of less than 1 year, providing for use and occupancy of premises." (Fla. Stat. 83.43(13))
- "This part does not apply to: ... Occupancy under a contract of sale of a dwelling unit or the property of which it is a part in which the buyer has paid at least 12 months’ rent or in which the buyer has paid at least 1 month’s rent and a deposit of at least 5 percent of the purchase price of the property." (Fla. Stat. 83.42)

## Cases

- [[Bodden v. Carbonell]], Fla. 2d DCA 1978, reading the 1975 statute: Was the agreement for the apartment complex laundry room a lease or a license?
- [[Solomon v. Gentry]], Fla. 4th DCA 1980, reading the 1979 statute: Was the rented mobile home a chattel, or real property held under a lease?
- [[Purcell v. Williams]], Fla. 1st DCA 1987, reading the 1985 statute: Was the agreement a contract for deed governed by the rules for mortgages, despite its clause applying the Landlord-Tenant Act on default?
- [[Frey v. Livecchi]], Fla. 4th DCA 2003, reading the 1999 statute: Did the court have to hold an evidentiary hearing on whether the occupants held under a contract for sale before ordering them to pay into the court registry?
- [[Grimm v. Huckabee]], Fla. 1st DCA 2005, reading the 2003 statute: Could the court evict under 83.60(2) without an evidentiary hearing on whether the occupants held under a contract for sale?
- [[Toledo v. Escamilla]], Fla. 3d DCA 2007, reading the 2004 statute: Was the occupant a tenant under the Act, so that the county court could proceed by eviction? Once the occupant answered that she was not a tenant and claimed an equitable interest in the property, which remedy and which court did the case belong to?
- [[Grant v. GHG014, LLC]], Fla. 4th DCA 2010, no statute year stated: Did the trial court abuse its discretion in denying the injunction because a landlord-tenant relationship had not been proved?
- [[Mesnikoff v. FQ Backyard Trading, LLC]], Fla. 3d DCA 2018, reading the 2016 statute: After the plaintiff dismissed its count for ejectment, could the county court enter a judgment of eviction? Had the complaint pleaded possession under 83.59(1), was there a landlord and tenant relationship for the county court to act on? Once the occupant answered that he held an equitable lien and ownership interest, was eviction or ejectment the remedy?

## How they relate

- Occupants who denied being tenants. Toledo and Mesnikoff, both Third District cases on second-tier certiorari, involve occupants who had lived with an owner, stayed after the unit was sold, and answered by claiming an interest in the property. Toledo: "Thus, a landlord/tenant relationship is a condition precedent to applying this statutory remedy." ([[Toledo v. Escamilla]] at 1030) Mesnikoff follows and quotes Toledo: "see also Toledo v. Escamilla, 962 So.2d 1028, 1030 (Fla. 3d DCA 2007) (holding that, because the party occupying the dwelling unit “is not a ‘tenant’ as defined by the Act, the county court lacked subject matter jurisdiction”)" ([[Mesnikoff v. FQ Backyard Trading, LLC]] at 770) On the remedy the two use the same words: "ejectment, not eviction, was the proper remedy, and the matter should have been transferred to the circuit court." ([[Toledo v. Escamilla]] at 1030) And: "ejectment, not eviction, was the proper remedy." ([[Mesnikoff v. FQ Backyard Trading, LLC]] at 770) In Mesnikoff the tenancy question comes after another ground, the dismissal of the only pleaded count: "Even if Backyard Trading did attempt to include in its complaint a second count for possession under section 83.59(1) of the Act, which it did not, we would nonetheless conclude that the county court lacked subject matter jurisdiction to enter a final judgment for eviction and possession because a landlord-tenant relationship did not exist." ([[Mesnikoff v. FQ Backyard Trading, LLC]] at 769)
- Occupants who said they were tenants. In Grant the positions were reversed: the occupants said they were tenants who had been locked out under 83.67(2), and the putative landlord denied any tenancy. "The threshold question for purposes of issuing the injunction was whether a landlord-tenant relationship ever came into existence." ([[Grant v. GHG014, LLC]] at 1067) The question came up on a motion for a mandatory injunction: "The tenants had the burden to establish their claim by evidence “clear and free from reasonable doubt,” which they did not do." ([[Grant v. GHG014, LLC]] at 1067) Grant states no statute year and cites none of the other cases here. The lockout: [[Self-help and wrongful eviction]].
- Occupants who said they were buyers. Frey, from the Fourth District, and Grimm, from the First, both arose from default judgments of eviction entered under 83.60. Frey: "However, section 83.60 does not apply when the occupancy is under a contract for sale of a dwelling unit or the property of which it is a part." ([[Frey v. Livecchi]] at 897) Grimm follows Frey: "We find the reasoning of Frey persuasive." ([[Grimm v. Huckabee]] at 610) Judge Benton, concurring in Grimm, spoke of the whole Act: "In the circumstances the Grimms allege, I agree that section 83.42(2), Florida Statutes (2003), renders the Florida Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, including section 83.60, Florida Statutes (2003), inapplicable." ([[Grimm v. Huckabee]] at 611) The registry side of both: [[Rent paid into the court registry]].
- The contract of sale exclusion changed. The 2003 text Grimm quotes ends with the property: "Section 83.42(2), Florida Statutes (2003), provides that “This part does not apply to: ... [o]ccupancy under a contract of sale of a dwelling unit or the property of which it is a part.”" ([[Grimm v. Huckabee]] at 610) Today's text, above, goes on: "in which the buyer has paid at least 12 months’ rent or in which the buyer has paid at least 1 month’s rent and a deposit of at least 5 percent of the purchase price of the property" (Fla. Stat. 83.42(2)).
- A contract for deed that invoked the Act. Purcell reads 697.01 on mortgages and cites no section of ch. 83, though the contract named the Act: "In case of default, the Landlord-Tenant Act will apply, as to a month-to-month tenancy and all monies paid shall be forfeited." ([[Purcell v. Williams]] at 1081) The court: "The trial court failed to recognize that the instrument in question is essentially a contract for deed which constituted a security transaction subject to the rules applicable to mortgages." ([[Purcell v. Williams]] at 1082) And: "As this purported lease provision was invalid, it provided no basis for the trial court to construe the agreement to be a lease or rental transaction rather than a mortgage transaction." ([[Purcell v. Williams]] at 1083) Frey and Grimm, also about occupants under contracts to buy, do not cite Purcell.
- The definitions then and today. Toledo read the 2004 text: "Section 83.43(4), Florida Statutes (2004), defines “tenant” as “any person entitled to occupy a dwelling unit under a rental agreement.”" ([[Toledo v. Escamilla]] at 1030) And: "Moreover, section 83.43(7) defines “rental agreement” as “any written agreement, or oral agreement if for less duration than 1 year, providing for use and occupancy of premises.”" ([[Toledo v. Escamilla]] at 1030) Its reasoning uses that duration: "Thus, any oral agreement between Ms. Toledo and her former boyfriend cannot be considered a “rental agreement” as defined in section 83.43(7), as the oral agreement, if any, between Ms. Toledo and her former boyfriend was not for a duration of less than one year." ([[Toledo v. Escamilla]] at 1030) Mesnikoff read the 2016 text under the same numbers, 83.43(3) for landlord and 83.43(4) for tenant, and quoted 83.41: "The Act “applies to the rental of a dwelling unit.”" ([[Mesnikoff v. FQ Backyard Trading, LLC]] at 770) Today's text, above, numbers the definitions of landlord, rental agreement, and tenant (9), (13), and (17), and its rental agreement definition adds "including amendments or addenda" (Fla. Stat. 83.43(13)).
- A mobile home. Solomon asked whether the rented mobile home was a chattel or real property: "Upon consideration of the factors enumerated above, we acknowledge that a mobile home may be classified either as a chattel or real property, depending upon the particular facts involved." ([[Solomon v. Gentry]] at 53) And: "we find that the mobile home in this context must be treated as real property and not as a chattel." ([[Solomon v. Gentry]] at 54) The tenants had argued the Act did not reach them: "By way of preliminary thesis, appellees argue that they were mere transient occupants of the mobile home, thereby precluding application of the Florida Residential Landlord and Tenant Act, Section 83.42(3) and 83.43(2)(b), Florida Statutes (1979)." ([[Solomon v. Gentry]] at 54) Today's dwelling unit definition includes "A mobile home rented by a tenant." (Fla. Stat. 83.43(5)(b)) and today's exclusions include "Transient occupancy in a hotel, condominium, motel, roominghouse, or similar public lodging, or transient occupancy in a mobile home park." (Fla. Stat. 83.42(3))
- A lease or a license. Bodden is a commercial case on a laundry room in an apartment complex, cites no section of ch. 83, and compares New York decisions: "In Polner and the case before us periodic rent was to be paid on the premises, the agreement was for a definite term of years, and the agreement was referred to as a lease." ([[Bodden v. Carbonell]] at 929) And: "In addition, in the instant case appellant had exclusive possession of a particularly described area in the apartment complex, and the parties to the agreement clearly intended to enter into a lease." ([[Bodden v. Carbonell]] at 929) None of the later cases here cites Bodden.
