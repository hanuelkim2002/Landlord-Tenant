# Mesnikoff v. FQ Backyard Trading, LLC

Third District, Chief Judge Rothenberg writing, on the occupant's petition for second-tier certiorari review of a circuit court appellate division's per curiam affirmance of a county court judgment of eviction. He had lived for years in a condominium titled to his late girlfriend, whose trustees sold it to the plaintiff.

## Facts

- "Mesnikoff and his girlfriend, Doris Dubler, began to live together in December 1989, after Dubler purchased a condominium titled solely in her name." (at 767)
- "On July 18, 2016, Kugler and Shader, individually and as co-trustees of the revocable trust, sold the condominium to Backyard Trading for $245,000." (at 767)
- "Importantly, the complaint made absolutely no reference to a tenancy; a rental agreement; or Chapter 83, Part II, Florida Statutes (2016), which is known as the Florida Residential Landlord and Tenant Act (“the Act”)." (at 767)

## After the plaintiff dismissed its count for ejectment, could the county court enter a judgment of eviction?

Topic: [[Whether there is a tenancy]]

The issue in the court's words: "Therefore, the sole issue before this Court is whether the circuit court failed to apply the correct law, resulting in a miscarriage of justice." (at 769)

Rule:
- "Our review of the complaint clearly indicates that Backyard Trading pled only one count—ejectment under section 66.021." (at 769)
- "Therefore, once Backyard Trading voluntarily dismissed its sole count for ejectment, the trial court lacked subject matter as there were no remaining claims to adjudicate" (at 769)
- "As the county court lacked subject matter jurisdiction, the decision of the circuit court, sitting in its appellate capacity, affirming the county court’s judgment of eviction constitutes a departure from the essential requirements of law resulting in a miscarriage of justice." (at 769)

Reasoning:
- "Backyard Trading then led the trial court astray by characterizing its complaint as a two-count complaint—(1) ejectment under section 66.021, and (2) possession—and by announcing that it was voluntarily dismissing the ejectment action and proceeding solely under its count for “possession.”" (at 769)
- In a footnote: "Even if Backyard Trading had not voluntarily dismissed its action for ejectment, the trial court would have nonetheless lacked subject matter jurisdiction because, as stated early, circuit courts have “exclusive original jurisdiction” in “actions for ejectment.”" (at 769)

## Had the complaint pleaded possession under 83.59(1), was there a landlord and tenant relationship for the county court to act on?

Topic: [[Whether there is a tenancy]]

Rule:
- "Even if Backyard Trading did attempt to include in its complaint a second count for possession under section 83.59(1) of the Act, which it did not, we would nonetheless conclude that the county court lacked subject matter jurisdiction to enter a final judgment for eviction and possession because a landlord-tenant relationship did not exist." (at 769)
- "Thus, section 83.59(1) of the Act does not apply." (at 770)

Reasoning:
- "Although we agree that Backyard Trading is a “landlord” under the Act, § 83.43(3), Fla. Stat. (2016) (defining “landlord” as “the owner or lessor of a dwelling unit”) (emphasis added), Mesnikoff is not a “tenant” under the Act because there was no rental agreement." (at 770)

## Once the occupant answered that he held an equitable lien and ownership interest, was eviction or ejectment the remedy?

Topic: [[Whether there is a tenancy]]

Rule:
- "In addition, we note that, based on his answer and affirmative defenses, which clearly indicated that Mesnikoff was not in possession of the condominium as a “tenant” and was claiming an equitable lien and ownership interest in the condominium, ejectment, not eviction, was the proper remedy." (at 770)
- "Accordingly, we would have also granted Mesnikoff’s second-tier certiorari petition for these separate reasons." (at 770)

Reasoning:
- The court quoted Toledo v. Escamilla, 962 So. 2d 1028 (Fla. 3d DCA 2007): "We also find that when [the party in possession of the dwelling unit] asserted in her answer that she was not a tenant and that she had an equitable interest in the property, ejectment, not eviction, was the proper remedy, and the matter should have been transferred to the circuit court" (at 770)
- Citing Ward v. Estate of Ward, 1 So. 3d 238 (Fla. 1st DCA 2008): "noting that “circuit courts have exclusive original jurisdiction in ejectment actions”" (at 770)

## Also said

- In a footnote: "Chapter 66 of the Florida Statutes pertains solely to actions for ejectment." (at 767)
- In a footnote, on review of a per curiam affirmance: "Although the circuit court, sitting in its appellate capacity, issued a per curiam affirmance in the present case, Mesnikoff was not precluded from seeking second-tier certiorari review in this Court." (at 768)

## Statute read

- The court quoted the 2016 text of 83.41: "The Act “applies to the rental of a dwelling unit.”" (at 770)
- And of 83.43(4): "defining “tenant” as “any person entitled to occupy a dwelling unit under a rental agreement”" (at 770)
